1. Equations of a Line in 3D Space (Parametric, Symmetric & Vector Forms)

To uniquely define a straight line $L$ in three-dimensional space ($\mathbb{R}^3$), we always need two essential ingredients:

  1. A Point on the Line: $P_0(x_0, y_0, z_0)$, with position vector $\mathbf{r}_0 = \langle x_0, y_0, z_0 \rangle$.
  2. A Parallel Direction Vector: $\vec{d} = \langle d_1, d_2, d_3 \rangle = d_1\mathbf{i} + d_2\mathbf{j} + d_3\mathbf{k}$ (often denoted $\mathbf{v} = \langle a, b, c \rangle$), which specifies the direction/tilt of the line in 3D space. The components $d_1, d_2, d_3$ are called the direction numbers of the line.

1.1 Vector Equation of a Line

For any arbitrary point $P(x, y, z)$ on the line $L$ with position vector $\mathbf{r} = \langle x, y, z \rangle$, the displacement vector $\overrightarrow{P_0 P} = \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_0$ must be parallel to $\vec{d}$, meaning $\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_0 = t\vec{d}$ for some scalar parameter $t \in (-\infty, \infty)$.

This gives the Vector Equation of the Line:

$$\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{r}_0 + t\vec{d} = \langle x_0, y_0, z_0 \rangle + t\langle d_1, d_2, d_3 \rangle = \langle x_0 + d_1 t,\; y_0 + d_2 t,\; z_0 + d_3 t \rangle$$

1.2 Parametric Equations of a Line

Equating the $x$-, $y$-, and $z$-components of the vector equation gives the Parametric Equations of a Line:

$$\begin{aligned} x &= x_0 + d_1 t \\ y &= y_0 + d_2 t \\ z &= z_0 + d_3 t \end{aligned} \qquad (-\infty < t < \infty)$$

where $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ is the known point $P_0$ on the line and $\langle d_1, d_2, d_3 \rangle$ are the components of the parallel direction vector $\vec{d}$.

1.3 Symmetric Equations of a Line

If none of the direction numbers $d_1, d_2, d_3$ is zero, we can eliminate the parameter $t$ by solving each parametric equation for $t$:

$$t = \frac{x - x_0}{d_1}, \qquad t = \frac{y - y_0}{d_2}, \qquad t = \frac{z - z_0}{d_3}$$

Equating these three expressions yields the Symmetric Equations of a Line:

$$\frac{x - x_0}{d_1} = \frac{y - y_0}{d_2} = \frac{z - z_0}{d_3}$$
  • Special Case (When a Direction Number is Zero): If one of the components of $\vec{d}$ is zero (for instance, $d_1 = 0$), we cannot divide by zero. Instead, $x$ remains constant at $x = x_0$, and the symmetric equations are written as:$$x = x_0, \qquad \frac{y - y_0}{d_2} = \frac{z - z_0}{d_3}$$
  • Shortcut Rule for Non-Standard Symmetric Equations: In standard symmetric form, the coefficients of $x, y, z$ in the numerators must all be $+1$. If a symmetric equation has a coefficient multiplying $x, y,$ or $z$ in the numerator (such as $\frac{2 - 3x}{6}$), the corresponding component of the direction vector $\vec{d}$ is found using the rule:$$d_i = \frac{\text{Denominator}}{\text{Coefficient of the variable in the numerator}}$$Algebraic justification: Factoring the coefficient $k$ out of $\frac{kx - x_1}{m}$ gives $\frac{k(x - x_1/k)}{m} = \frac{x - x_1/k}{m/k}$, so the standard denominator is indeed $\frac{m}{k}$.

1.4 Line Through Two Points & Line Segments

  • Line Passing Through Two Points $P_1(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $P_2(x_2, y_2, z_2)$: Neither the direction vector nor the equation is given directly, so we construct the direction vector as the vector connecting the two points:$$\vec{d} = \overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} = \langle x_2 - x_1,\; y_2 - y_1,\; z_2 - z_1 \rangle \quad \text{or} \quad \vec{d} = \overrightarrow{P_2 P_1} = \langle x_1 - x_2,\; y_1 - y_2,\; z_1 - z_2 \rangle$$Either $P_1$ or $P_2$ can then be used as the point $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$.
  • Equation of a Line Segment: The finite line segment from $\mathbf{r}_0$ to $\mathbf{r}_1$ is parameterized by restricting $t$ to $[0, 1]$:$$\mathbf{r}(t) = (1 - t)\mathbf{r}_0 + t\mathbf{r}_1 = \mathbf{r}_0 + t(\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_0), \qquad 0 \le t \le 1$$

Question 1: Find Parametric Equations Given a Point and a Parallel Vector

Problem

Find the parametric equations of the line that passes through the point $P(2, -1, 3)$ and is parallel to the vector $\vec{d} = 2\mathbf{i} - 3\mathbf{j} + 4\mathbf{k}$.

Final Answer

$$x = 2 + 2t, \qquad y = -1 - 3t, \qquad z = 3 + 4t$$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Identify the given point $P_0(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ and direction vector $\vec{d} = \langle d_1, d_2, d_3 \rangle$.

The line passes through the point $P_0(2, -1, 3)$, so:

$$x_0 = 2, \qquad y_0 = -1, \qquad z_0 = 3$$

The line is parallel to the vector $\vec{d} = 2\mathbf{i} - 3\mathbf{j} + 4\mathbf{k}$. Writing $\vec{d}$ in component form gives:

$$\vec{d} = \langle d_1, d_2, d_3 \rangle = \langle 2, -3, 4 \rangle \implies d_1 = 2, \quad d_2 = -3, \quad d_3 = 4$$

Step 2: Substitute into the parametric equations formula.

Recall the general parametric equations of a line in $\mathbb{R}^3$:

$$\begin{aligned} x &= x_0 + d_1 t \\ y &= y_0 + d_2 t \\ z &= z_0 + d_3 t \end{aligned}$$

Substituting $(x_0, y_0, z_0) = (2, -1, 3)$ and $\langle d_1, d_2, d_3 \rangle = \langle 2, -3, 4 \rangle$:

$$\begin{aligned} x &= 2 + 2t \\ y &= -1 + (-3)t = -1 - 3t \\ z &= 3 + 4t \end{aligned}$$

where $t \in \mathbb{R}$ is the parameter.

Question 2: Write Symmetric Equations Given a Point and a Direction Vector

Problem

Write the symmetric equations of the line passing through the point $P(2, -1, 3)$ with direction vector $\vec{d} = \langle 2, -3, 4 \rangle$.

Final Answer

$$\frac{x - 2}{2} = \frac{y + 1}{-3} = \frac{z - 3}{4}$$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Recall the symmetric equations formula.

For a line passing through $P_0(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ with direction vector $\vec{d} = \langle d_1, d_2, d_3 \rangle$ (where $d_1, d_2, d_3 \neq 0$), the symmetric equations are:

$$\frac{x - x_0}{d_1} = \frac{y - y_0}{d_2} = \frac{z - z_0}{d_3}$$

Step 2: Substitute the coordinates of $P(2, -1, 3)$ and the components of $\vec{d} = \langle 2, -3, 4 \rangle$.

Here, $(x_0, y_0, z_0) = (2, -1, 3)$ goes into the numerators (with a minus sign from the formula), and $(d_1, d_2, d_3) = (2, -3, 4)$ goes into the denominators:

$$\frac{x - 2}{2} = \frac{y - (-1)}{-3} = \frac{z - 3}{4}$$

Simplifying $y - (-1)$ to $y + 1$ gives the final symmetric equations:

$$\frac{x - 2}{2} = \frac{y + 1}{-3} = \frac{z - 3}{4}$$

Question 3: Parametric Equations of a Line Parallel to a Given Symmetric Line

Problem

Find the parametric equations of the line that passes through the point $P(4, 3, -1)$ and is parallel to the line:

$$L: \frac{x - 3}{2} = \frac{y - 2}{-4} = 3z$$
Final Answer

Standard Exact Form: $$x = 4 + 2t, \qquad y = 3 - 4t, \qquad z = -1 + \frac{1}{3}t$$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Understand the geometric meaning of parallel lines.

Two lines are parallel in 3D space if and only if their direction vectors are parallel (proportional).

Therefore, we can use the direction vector $\vec{d}$ of the given line $L$ as the direction vector for our new line.

Step 2: Extract the direction vector $\vec{d}$ from the symmetric equation of $L$.

The given line is:

$$\frac{x - 3}{2} = \frac{y - 2}{-4} = \frac{3z}{1}$$

$$d_i = \frac{\text{Denominator}}{\text{Coefficient of the variable in the numerator}}$$

(If the problem statement intended $\frac{z}{3}$ instead of $3z$, then $d_3 = 3$.)

Using the point $P_0(4, 3, -1)$:

  • With the exact algebraic direction vector $\vec{d} = \langle 2, -4, 1/3 \rangle$:$$x = 4 + 2t, \qquad y = 3 - 4t, \qquad z = -1 + \frac{1}{3}t$$
  • With the whiteboard vector $\vec{d}_{\text{board}} = \langle 2, -4, 3 \rangle$:$$x = 4 + 2t, \qquad y = 3 - 4t, \qquad z = -1 + 3t$$

Question 4: Parametric Equations When the Parallel Line Has Non-Standard Numerators

Problem

Find the parametric equations of the line passing through the point $P(1, -2, 3)$ and parallel to the line:

$$L: \frac{2 - 3x}{6} = \frac{1 - y}{4} = z$$
Final Answer

$$x = 1 - 2t, \qquad y = -2 - 4t, \qquad z = 3 + t$$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Identify the point on the line.

The given point is $P_0(1, -2, 3)$, so $x_0 = 1$, $y_0 = -2$, and $z_0 = 3$.

Step 2: Extract the direction vector $\vec{d}$ from the parallel line $L$.

Notice that the symmetric equations of $L$ are not in standard form because the coefficients of $x$ and $y$ in the numerators are $-3$ and $-1$, respectively, rather than $+1$:

$$\frac{2 - 3x}{6} = \frac{1 - y}{4} = \frac{z}{1}$$

Applying the rule $d_i = \frac{\text{Denominator}}{\text{Coefficient of variable}}$:

  • $x$-component ($d_1$): The denominator is $6$ and the coefficient of $x$ is $-3$:$$d_1 = \frac{6}{-3} = -2$$
  • $y$-component ($d_2$): The denominator is $4$ and the coefficient of $y$ is $-1$:$$d_2 = \frac{4}{-1} = -4$$
  • $z$-component ($d_3$): Since $z = \frac{z}{1}$, the denominator is $1$ and the coefficient of $z$ is $1$:$$d_3 = \frac{1}{1} = 1$$

Thus, the direction vector is:

$$\vec{d} = \langle -2, -4, 1 \rangle$$

Step 3: Substitute $P_0(1, -2, 3)$ and $\vec{d} = \langle -2, -4, 1 \rangle$ into the parametric form.

$$\begin{aligned} x &= x_0 + d_1 t = 1 - 2t \\ y &= y_0 + d_2 t = -2 - 4t \\ z &= z_0 + d_3 t = 3 + t \end{aligned}$$

Question 5: Find the Symmetric Equations of a Line Passing Through Two Points

Problem

Find the symmetric equations of the line passing through the two points $P_1(2, 1, 0)$ and $P_2(-1, 4, 3)$.

Final Answer

$$\frac{x - 2}{-3} = \frac{y - 1}{3} = \frac{z}{3} \qquad \left(\text{or equivalently } \frac{x - 2}{-1} = \frac{y - 1}{1} = \frac{z}{1}\right)$$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Find the direction vector $\vec{d}$ from the two points.

When a line passes through two points $P_1(2, 1, 0)$ and $P_2(-1, 4, 3)$, the displacement vector $\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2}$ lies directly along the line and serves as its direction vector $\vec{d}$:

$$\vec{d} = \overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} = \langle x_2 - x_1,\; y_2 - y_1,\; z_2 - z_1 \rangle = \langle -1 - 2,\; 4 - 1,\; 3 - 0 \rangle = \langle -3, 3, 3 \rangle$$

Remark: You could equally choose $\vec{d} = \overrightarrow{P_2 P_1} = \langle 2 - (-1),\; 1 - 4,\; 0 - 3 \rangle = \langle 3, -3, -3 \rangle$, or divide by $3$ to get $\langle -1, 1, 1 \rangle$, because any non-zero scalar multiple of $\vec{d}$ points along the same line.

Step 2: Choose one of the two points and write the symmetric equations.

We can choose either $P_1(2, 1, 0)$ or $P_2(-1, 4, 3)$ as our reference point $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$. Choosing $P_1(2, 1, 0)$ and $\vec{d} = \langle -3, 3, 3 \rangle$:

$$\frac{x - x_0}{d_1} = \frac{y - y_0}{d_2} = \frac{z - z_0}{d_3} \implies \frac{x - 2}{-3} = \frac{y - 1}{3} = \frac{z - 0}{3}$$

Simplifying $z - 0$ to $z$ gives:

$$\frac{x - 2}{-3} = \frac{y - 1}{3} = \frac{z}{3}$$
Explanation

2. Relationships Between Two Lines in 3D Space: Parallel, Intersecting, or Skew

In two-dimensional space ($\mathbb{R}^2$), any two lines are either parallel or they intersect.

In three-dimensional space ($\mathbb{R}^3$), however, there is a third possibility because two lines can lie in different planes and pass over/under one another without ever touching.

Given two lines $L_1$ (with direction vector $\vec{d}_1 = \langle a_1, b_1, c_1 \rangle$) and $L_2$ (with direction vector $\vec{d}_2 = \langle a_2, b_2, c_2 \rangle$), there are three possible relationships:

  1. Parallel Lines ($L_1 \parallel L_2$): Their direction vectors are parallel (scalar multiples of each other, $\vec{d}_1 = k\vec{d}_2$):$$\frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{c_1}{c_2}$$
  2. Intersecting Lines: They are not parallel, and they share a unique common intersection point $(x, y, z)$ in 3D space.
  3. Skew Lines: They are not parallel AND they do not intersect (they do not lie in the same plane).

Systematic 2-Step Algorithm to Classify $L_1$ and $L_2$

  • Step 1: Check Parallelism.
    Extract the direction vectors $\vec{d}_1 = \langle a_1, b_1, c_1 \rangle$ and $\vec{d}_2 = \langle a_2, b_2, c_2 \rangle$ and test whether their components are proportional:$$\frac{a_1}{a_2} \stackrel{?}{=} \frac{b_1}{b_2} \stackrel{?}{=} \frac{c_1}{c_2}$$If all three ratios are equal, $L_1$ and $L_2$ are parallel ($L_1 \parallel L_2$). If the ratios are not equal, the lines are not parallel; proceed to Step 2.
  • Step 2: Check Intersection vs. Skew.
    1. Ensure both lines are written in parametric form using different parameter names ($t$ for $L_1$ and $s$ for $L_2$). If they are given in symmetric form, convert them to parametric form first.
    2. Equate the $x$-coordinates ($x_1 = x_2$) and the $y$-coordinates ($y_1 = y_2$) to obtain two equations in the two unknowns $t$ and $s$.
    3. Solve this $2 \times 2$ linear system to find the values of $t$ and $s$.
    4. Test in $z$: Substitute the found values of $t$ and $s$ into $z_1$ and $z_2$:
      • If $z_1 = z_2$, the lines intersect! Substitute $t$ into $L_1$ (or $s$ into $L_2$) to get the intersection point $(x, y, z)$.
      • If $z_1 \neq z_2$, the system has no solution, so the lines are skew lines!

Angle Between Two Intersecting Lines (Syllabus Note)

The acute angle $\theta$ between two lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ is the angle between their direction vectors $\vec{d}_1$ and $\vec{d}_2$:

$$\cos\theta = \frac{|\vec{d}_1 \cdot \vec{d}_2|}{\|\vec{d}_1\|\,\|\vec{d}_2\|}$$

In particular, $L_1 \perp L_2$ (orthogonal/perpendicular) if and only if $\vec{d}_1 \cdot \vec{d}_2 = 0$.

Question 6: Testing Two Parametric Lines in Space

Problem

Determine whether the lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ are parallel, intersecting, or skew lines. If they intersect, find the point of intersection:

$$L_1: x = 2 + t, \qquad y = 3 - 2t, \qquad z = 1 - 3t$$$$L_2: x = 3 + s, \qquad y = -4 + 3s, \qquad z = 2 - 7s$$
Final Answer

The lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ are intersecting lines, and their point of intersection is $(4, -1, -5)$.

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Check whether $L_1$ and $L_2$ are parallel.

From the coefficients of the parameters $t$ and $s$, we read off the direction vectors $\vec{d}_1$ and $\vec{d}_2$:

$$\vec{d}_1 = \langle 1, -2, -3 \rangle, \qquad \vec{d}_2 = \langle 1, 3, -7 \rangle$$

Now check the ratios of corresponding components:

$$\frac{1}{1} \neq \frac{-2}{3} \neq \frac{-3}{-7}$$

Since the ratios are not equal, $\vec{d}_1$ and $\vec{d}_2$ are not proportional. Therefore, $L_1$ and $L_2$ are not parallel.

Step 2: Check whether $L_1$ and $L_2$ intersect.

We equate the $x$-coordinates ($x_1 = x_2$) and $y$-coordinates ($y_1 = y_2$) to solve for $t$ and $s$:

  • From $x_1 = x_2$:$$2 + t = 3 + s \implies t = 1 + s \qquad \text{--- (Equation 1)}$$
  • From $y_1 = y_2$:$$3 - 2t = -4 + 3s$$

Substitute $t = 1 + s$ from Equation (1) into the $y$-equation:

$$\begin{aligned} 3 - 2(1 + s) &= -4 + 3s \\ 3 - 2 - 2s &= -4 + 3s \\ 1 - 2s &= -4 + 3s \\ 1 + 4 &= 3s + 2s \\ 5 &= 5s \implies \boxed{s = 1} \end{aligned}$$

Now substitute $s = 1$ back into Equation (1) to find $t$:

$$t = 1 + s = 1 + 1 \implies \boxed{t = 2}$$

Step 3: Check consistency in the $z$-coordinates ($z_1 \stackrel{?}{=} z_2$).

Substitute $t = 2$ into $z_1$ and $s = 1$ into $z_2$:

$$\begin{aligned} z_1 &= 1 - 3(2) = 1 - 6 = -5 \\ z_2 &= 2 - 7(1) = 2 - 7 = -5 \end{aligned}$$

Since $z_1 = z_2 = -5$, the third coordinate matches! Therefore, the lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ intersect.

Step 4: Find the point of intersection $(x, y, z)$.

Substitute $t = 2$ into the parametric equations of $L_1$ (or $s = 1$ into $L_2$):

$$\begin{aligned} x &= 2 + t = 2 + 2 = 4 \\ y &= 3 - 2t = 3 - 2(2) = -1 \\ z &= 1 - 3t = 1 - 3(2) = -5 \end{aligned}$$

Thus, the point of intersection is $(4, -1, -5)$.

Question 7: Testing Two Parametric Lines in Space

Problem

Determine whether the lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ are parallel, intersecting, or skew lines:

$$L_1: x = 1 + 4t, \qquad y = 2 + 4t, \qquad z = 1 + 8t$$$$L_2: x = 1 + 8s, \qquad y = 3 + 8s, \qquad z = 1 + 16s$$
Final Answer

The lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ are parallel lines ($L_1 \parallel L_2$).

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Extract the direction vectors $\vec{d}_1$ and $\vec{d}_2$.

From the coefficients of $t$ in $L_1$ and $s$ in $L_2$:

$$\vec{d}_1 = \langle 4, 4, 8 \rangle, \qquad \vec{d}_2 = \langle 8, 8, 16 \rangle$$

Step 2: Check the ratios of corresponding components.

$$\frac{4}{8} = \frac{4}{8} = \frac{8}{16} = \frac{1}{2}$$

Since all three ratios are equal to $\frac{1}{2}$ (meaning $\vec{d}_2 = 2\vec{d}_1$), the direction vectors are scalar multiples of one another.

Therefore, $L_1$ and $L_2$ are parallel ($L_1 \parallel L_2$), and no further steps are needed.

Additional Check (Distinct Parallel vs. Identical): At $t = 0$, $L_1$ passes through $(1, 2, 1)$.

For $L_2$ to have $x = 1$ and $z = 1$, we need $s = 0$, which gives $y = 3 \neq 2$. Thus, $L_1$ and $L_2$ are distinct parallel lines.

Question 8: Classifying Lines Given in Symmetric Form

Problem

Determine whether the lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ given in symmetric form are parallel, intersecting, or skew:

$$L_1: \frac{x - 2}{1} = \frac{y - 3}{-2} = \frac{z - 1}{-3}$$$$L_2: \frac{x - 3}{1} = \frac{y + 4}{3} = \frac{z - 2}{-7}$$

(Also explain how the result would change if the third ratio of $L_2$ were $\frac{z - 4}{-7}$ instead.)

Final Answer

Converted to parametric form, $L_1$ and $L_2$ are identical to Question 6, so they intersect at $(4, -1, -5)$. If the third ratio of $L_2$ were $\frac{z - 4}{-7}$, then $z_1 = -5 \neq z_2 = -3$, making them skew lines.

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Convert both symmetric equations into parametric form.

Whenever two lines are given in symmetric form, the easiest way to test for intersection or skewness is to rewrite both lines in parametric form using parameter $t$ for $L_1$ and parameter $s$ for $L_2$:

  • For $L_1$: Set all three ratios equal to $t$:$$\frac{x - 2}{1} = \frac{y - 3}{-2} = \frac{z - 1}{-3} = t$$The point on $L_1$ is $(2, 3, 1)$ (flipping the signs in the numerators) and the direction vector is $\vec{d}_1 = \langle 1, -2, -3 \rangle$ (from the denominators), giving:$$L_1: x = 2 + t, \qquad y = 3 - 2t, \qquad z = 1 - 3t$$
  • For $L_2$: Set all three ratios equal to $s$:$$\frac{x - 3}{1} = \frac{y + 4}{3} = \frac{z - 2}{-7} = s$$The point on $L_2$ is $(3, -4, 2)$ and the direction vector is $\vec{d}_2 = \langle 1, 3, -7 \rangle$, giving:$$L_2: x = 3 + s, \qquad y = -4 + 3s, \qquad z = 2 - 7s$$

Step 2: Apply the 2-Step Classification Algorithm.

Notice that these parametric equations are the exact same equations solved in Question 6:

  1. Parallel Check: $\vec{d}_1 = \langle 1, -2, -3 \rangle$ and $\vec{d}_2 = \langle 1, 3, -7 \rangle$ are not proportional ($\frac{1}{1} \neq \frac{-2}{3}$), so the lines are not parallel.
  2. Intersection Check: Solving $x_1 = x_2$ ($2 + t = 3 + s$) and $y_1 = y_2$ ($3 - 2t = -4 + 3s$) yields $s = 1$ and $t = 2$.
  3. $z$-Check: Substituting $t = 2$ and $s = 1$ gives $z_1 = 1 - 3(2) = -5$ and $z_2 = 2 - 7(1) = -5$. Since $z_1 = z_2$, the lines intersect at $(4, -1, -5)$.

Step 3: How Skew Lines Arise (Illustrative Comparison).

Suppose the third symmetric ratio of $L_2$ had been $\frac{z - 4}{-7}$ instead of $\frac{z - 2}{-7}$, so that $z_2 = 4 - 7s$. Then:

  • The direction vectors $\vec{d}_1 = \langle 1, -2, -3 \rangle$ and $\vec{d}_2 = \langle 1, 3, -7 \rangle$ are still not parallel.
  • Equating $x_1 = x_2$ and $y_1 = y_2$ still gives $s = 1$ and $t = 2$.
  • However, when we check the $z$-coordinates at $t = 2$ and $s = 1$:$$z_1 = 1 - 3(2) = -5, \qquad z_2 = 4 - 7(1) = -3$$Since $z_1 \neq z_2$ ($-5 \neq -3$), there is no intersection point. Because the lines are neither parallel nor intersecting, they are skew lines!

1. Vector, Scalar & Linear Equations of a Plane

In three-dimensional space ($\mathbb{R}^3$), a flat surface extending infinitely in all directions is called a plane (often denoted by $\pi$).

1. Special Coordinate & Coordinate-Parallel Planes

  • $x = a$: A plane perpendicular to the $x$-axis (parallel to the $yz$-plane) passing through $(a, 0, 0)$. In particular, $x = 0$ is the $yz$-plane.
  • $y = a$: A plane perpendicular to the $y$-axis (parallel to the $xz$-plane) passing through $(0, a, 0)$. In particular, $y = 0$ is the $xz$-plane.
  • $z = a$: A plane perpendicular to the $z$-axis (parallel to the $xy$-plane) passing through $(0, 0, a)$. In particular, $z = 0$ is the $xy$-plane.

2. What Defines a General Plane in 3D Space?

While a line in 3D space is determined by a point and a parallel direction vector $\mathbf{d}$, a single parallel vector is not enough to fix the tilt of a plane.

Instead, a plane $\pi$ is uniquely determined by:

  1. A known point on the plane: $P_0(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ (with position vector $\mathbf{r}_0 = \langle x_0, y_0, z_0 \rangle$).
  2. A non-zero normal vector $\mathbf{n} = \langle a, b, c \rangle$ that is perpendicular (orthogonal) to the plane ($\mathbf{n} \perp \pi$).

3. Vector, Scalar (Point-Normal) & Linear Equations of a Plane

  • Vector Equation: Let $P(x, y, z)$ be any arbitrary point on the plane with position vector $\mathbf{r} = \langle x, y, z \rangle$. The vector $\overrightarrow{P_0 P} = \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_0 = \langle x - x_0, y - y_0, z - z_0 \rangle$ lies completely inside the plane. Because the normal vector $\mathbf{n}$ is perpendicular to every vector in the plane, their dot product must equal zero: $$\mathbf{n} \cdot (\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_0) = 0$$
  • Scalar (Standard / Point-Normal) Equation: Computing the dot product $\langle a, b, c \rangle \cdot \langle x - x_0, y - y_0, z - z_0 \rangle = 0$ yields the standard equation of a plane: $$a(x - x_0) + b(y - y_0) + c(z - z_0) = 0$$
  • Linear (General) Equation: Expanding the parentheses and combining the constant terms into $d = -(ax_0 + by_0 + cz_0)$ gives the general linear equation of a plane: $$ax + by + cz + d = 0$$

4. Reading the Normal Vector from a Plane Equation

Whenever a plane is given in linear form $ax + by + cz + d = 0$ (or $ax + by + cz = k$), the coefficients of $x$, $y$, and $z$ immediately give the components of the plane's normal vector $\mathbf{n} = \langle a, b, c \rangle$.

Board Example: Given the plane equation

$$3x - 2y + 5z - 3 = 0$$

its normal vector is read directly from the coefficients of $x, y, z$:

$$\mathbf{n} = \langle 3, -2, 5 \rangle$$

2. Finding Planes via Cross Product, Parallel & Orthogonal Planes

In many problems, the normal vector $\mathbf{n}$ is not given directly.

Instead, you are given geometric conditions (such as three points on the plane, two lines in the plane, or planes perpendicular to the desired plane).

The key strategy is to find two non-parallel vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ that lie in (or are parallel to) the plane, and then take their cross product:

$$\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$$

Because $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$ is orthogonal to both $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$, it is guaranteed to be orthogonal to the entire plane!

Summary of Geometric Configurations

  1. Plane Containing Three Non-Collinear Points $P_1, P_2, P_3$:
    • Form two vectors connecting the points inside the plane, for example $\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2}$ and $\overrightarrow{P_2 P_3}$ (or $\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2}$ and $\overrightarrow{P_1 P_3}$).
    • Compute the normal vector: $\mathbf{n} = \overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} \times \overrightarrow{P_2 P_3}$.
    • Use any of the three points ($P_1$, $P_2$, or $P_3$) with $\mathbf{n}$ to write the plane equation.
  2. Plane Containing Two Intersecting Lines $L_1$ and $L_2$:
    • Since both lines lie in the plane, their direction vectors $\mathbf{d}_1$ and $\mathbf{d}_2$ lie in the plane.
    • Compute the normal vector: $\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2$.
    • Choose a point $P_0$ from either line $L_1$ (by setting $t = 0$) or $L_2$ (by setting $s = 0$).
  3. Plane Parallel to Another Plane:
    • If two planes $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$ are parallel ($\pi_1 \parallel \pi_2$), a vector perpendicular to one is automatically perpendicular to the other. Thus, they share the same normal vector: $$\mathbf{n}_1 = \mathbf{n}_2$$
  4. Plane Perpendicular to Two Given Planes $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$:
    • If the desired plane $\pi_3$ is perpendicular to both $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$, then the normal vector $\mathbf{n}_3$ must be perpendicular to both $\mathbf{n}_1$ and $\mathbf{n}_2$ (since $\mathbf{n}_1$ and $\mathbf{n}_2$ are parallel to $\pi_3$).
    • Compute the normal vector: $\mathbf{n}_3 = \mathbf{n}_1 \times \mathbf{n}_2$.

Question 2: Equation of a Plane Containing Three Points

Problem

Find the equation of the plane that contains the points $P_1(-1, 2, 0)$, $P_2(3, 1, 1)$, and $P_3(0, 4, 4)$.

Final Answer

$$-6(x + 1) - 15(y - 2) + 9z = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad 2x + 5y - 3z - 8 = 0$$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Construct two vectors lying in the plane from the three given points.

Since $P_1(-1, 2, 0)$, $P_2(3, 1, 1)$, and $P_3(0, 4, 4)$ all lie in the plane, the displacement vectors $\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2}$ and $\overrightarrow{P_2 P_3}$ lie entirely inside the plane:

$$\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} = P_2 - P_1 = \langle 3 - (-1), \; 1 - 2, \; 1 - 0 \rangle = \langle 4, -1, 1 \rangle$$ $$\overrightarrow{P_2 P_3} = P_3 - P_2 = \langle 0 - 3, \; 4 - 1, \; 4 - 1 \rangle = \langle -3, 3, 3 \rangle$$

(Note: You could also use $\overrightarrow{P_1 P_3} = \langle 1, 2, 4 \rangle$; any pair of vectors between the three points yields the same normal direction.)

Step 2: Compute the normal vector $\mathbf{n}$ using the cross product.

The normal vector $\mathbf{n}$ is perpendicular to both $\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2}$ and $\overrightarrow{P_2 P_3}$:

$$\mathbf{n} = \overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} \times \overrightarrow{P_2 P_3} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ 4 & -1 & 1 \\ -3 & 3 & 3 \end{vmatrix}$$

Expanding the determinant by cofactors along the first row:

  • $\mathbf{i}$-component: $+\big((-1)(3) - (1)(3)\big) = -3 - 3 = -6$
  • $\mathbf{j}$-component: $-\big((4)(3) - (1)(-3)\big) = -(12 + 3) = -15$
  • $\mathbf{k}$-component: $+\big((4)(3) - (-1)(-3)\big) = 12 - 3 = 9$

Thus, the normal vector is:

$$\mathbf{n} = \langle -6, -15, 9 \rangle$$

Step 3: Substitute $\mathbf{n}$ and one of the given points into the scalar plane equation.

We can choose any of $P_1$, $P_2$, or $P_3$. Choosing $P_1(-1, 2, 0)$ (since it has a $0$ coordinate, making arithmetic simpler):

$$\pi: \quad -6(x - (-1)) - 15(y - 2) + 9(z - 0) = 0$$ $$\pi: \quad -6(x + 1) - 15(y - 2) + 9z = 0$$

Step 4: Simplify to general linear form.

$$-6x - 6 - 15y + 30 + 9z = 0 \implies -6x - 15y + 9z + 24 = 0$$

Dividing the entire equation by $-3$ gives the cleanest equivalent form:

$$2x + 5y - 3z - 8 = 0$$

Question 4: Equation of a Plane Containing Two Lines

Problem

Find the equation of the plane that contains the two lines:

$$L_1: \; x = 1 + 2t, \quad y = 5 + t, \quad z = 3t$$$$L_2: \; x = 5 + 4s, \quad y = -2s, \quad z = 1 + 6s$$
Final Answer

$$12(x - 1) - 8z = 0 \quad \text{(or } 3x - 2z - 3 = 0\text{)}$$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Extract the direction vectors of both lines.

From the coefficients of the parameters $t$ and $s$, the direction vectors of $L_1$ and $L_2$ are:

$$\mathbf{d}_1 = \langle 2, 1, 3 \rangle$$ $$\mathbf{d}_2 = \langle 4, -2, 6 \rangle$$

Step 2: Find the normal vector of the plane using the cross product.

Since the plane contains both lines $L_1$ and $L_2$, both direction vectors $\mathbf{d}_1$ and $\mathbf{d}_2$ lie in the plane.

Because $\mathbf{d}_1$ and $\mathbf{d}_2$ are not parallel (the ratios $\frac{4}{2} = 2$ and $\frac{-2}{1} = -2$ are not equal), their cross product gives a normal vector $\mathbf{n}$ perpendicular to the plane:

$$\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2 = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 4 & -2 & 6 \end{vmatrix}$$
  • $\mathbf{i}$-component: $+\big((1)(6) - (3)(-2)\big) = 6 + 6 = 12$
  • $\mathbf{j}$-component: $-\big((2)(6) - (3)(4)\big) = -(12 - 12) = 0$
  • $\mathbf{k}$-component: $+\big((2)(-2) - (1)(4)\big) = -4 - 4 = -8$
$$\mathbf{n} = \langle 12, 0, -8 \rangle$$

Step 3: Extract a point on the plane from either line.

Every point on $L_1$ and $L_2$ lies on the plane. Setting $t = 0$ in the equations of $L_1$ gives the point:

$$P(1, 5, 0)$$

(Alternatively, setting $s = 0$ in $L_2$ gives the point $(5, 0, 1)$.)

Step 4: Write the equation of the plane.

Using $P(1, 5, 0)$ and $\mathbf{n} = \langle 12, 0, -8 \rangle$:

$$\pi: \quad 12(x - 1) + 0(y - 5) - 8(z - 0) = 0$$ $$12(x - 1) - 8z = 0 \implies 3x - 2z - 3 = 0$$

Question 6: Equation of a Plane Passing Through a Point and Perpendicular to Two Planes

Problem

Find the equation of the plane that passes through the point $(1, 5, 1)$ and is perpendicular to both planes $\pi_1: \; 2x + y - 2z = 2$ and $\pi_2: \; x + 3z = 4$.

$$3(x - 1) - 8(y - 5) - (z - 1) = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad 3x - 8y - z + 38 = 0$$

Step 1: Extract the normal vectors of the two given planes $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$.

$$\mathbf{n}_3 = \langle 3, -8, -1 \rangle$$

$$3(x - 1) - 8(y - 5) - 1(z - 1) = 0$$

Expanding to general linear form:

$$3x - 3 - 8y + 40 - z + 1 = 0 \implies 3x - 8y - z + 38 = 0$$

3. Intersections & Angles Between Lines and Planes

1. Point of Intersection Between a Line and a Plane

Given a line in parametric form $x = x(t), \; y = y(t), \; z = z(t)$ and a plane $ax + by + cz + d = 0$:

  1. Substitute $x(t)$, $y(t)$, and $z(t)$ from the line directly into the plane equation.
  2. Solve the resulting linear equation for the parameter $t$.
  3. Substitute $t$ back into the parametric equations of the line to obtain the coordinates $(x, y, z)$ of the intersection point.

2. Line of Intersection Between Two Planes

When two non-parallel planes $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$ intersect, their intersection is a straight line $L$.

  • Method 1 (Two-Point Method shown on the board):
    1. Find a first point $P_1$ on the line of intersection by setting one coordinate to zero (e.g., $z = 0$) in both plane equations and solving the resulting $2 \times 2$ system for $x$ and $y$.
    2. Find a second point $P_2$ on the line of intersection by setting a different coordinate to zero (e.g., $x = 0$) in both plane equations and solving the $2 \times 2$ system for $y$ and $z$.
    3. Compute the direction vector of the line connecting the two points: $\mathbf{d} = \overrightarrow{P_1 P_2}$.
    4. Write the parametric equations of the line using $P_1$ (or $P_2$) and $\mathbf{d}$.
  • Method 2 (Cross-Product + One-Point Method):
    • Since the line of intersection lies inside both planes $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$, its direction vector $\mathbf{d}$ is orthogonal to both normal vectors $\mathbf{n}_1$ and $\mathbf{n}_2$: $$\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{n}_1 \times \mathbf{n}_2$$
    • Find just one point $P_1$ on both planes (e.g., by setting $z = 0$) and form the line equation.

3. Angles Between Planes and Lines

  • Angle Between Two Planes ($\pi_1, \pi_2$): Equals the angle between their normal vectors $\mathbf{n}_1$ and $\mathbf{n}_2$: $$\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{n}_1 \cdot \mathbf{n}_2}{\|\mathbf{n}_1\| \|\mathbf{n}_2\|} \quad \left(\text{use } \frac{|\mathbf{n}_1 \cdot \mathbf{n}_2|}{\|\mathbf{n}_1\| \|\mathbf{n}_2\|} \text{ for the acute angle}\right)$$
  • Angle Between Two Lines ($L_1, L_2$): Equals the angle between their direction vectors $\mathbf{d}_1$ and $\mathbf{d}_2$: $$\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{d}_1 \cdot \mathbf{d}_2}{\|\mathbf{d}_1\| \|\mathbf{d}_2\|}$$
  • Angle Between a Line $L$ and a Plane $\pi$: Because the normal vector $\mathbf{n}$ makes an angle of $\frac{\pi}{2}$ ($90^\circ$) with the plane, the angle between a line (direction $\mathbf{d}$) and a plane (normal $\mathbf{n}$) is the complement of the angle between $\mathbf{n}$ and $\mathbf{d}$: $$\text{Angle}(L, \pi) = \frac{\pi}{2} - \text{Angle}(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{d})$$

Question 7: Point of Intersection Between a Line and a Plane

Problem

Find the point of intersection between the line $x = 1 + 2t, \; y = 4t, \; z = 2 - 3t$ and the plane $x + 2y - z + 1 = 0$.

Final Answer

$$t = 0 \implies \text{Point of Intersection: } (1, 0, 2)$$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Substitute the parametric equations of the line into the plane equation.

At the point of intersection, the coordinates $(x, y, z)$ of the line must satisfy the plane equation $x + 2y - z + 1 = 0$.

Replacing $x$ with $(1 + 2t)$, $y$ with $(4t)$, and $z$ with $(2 - 3t)$:

$$(1 + 2t) + 2(4t) - (2 - 3t) + 1 = 0$$

Step 2: Simplify and solve for the parameter $t$.

Distribute the signs carefully (especially the minus sign before $(2 - 3t)$):

$$1 + 2t + 8t - 2 + 3t + 1 = 0$$

Combine the $t$-terms $(2t + 8t + 3t = 13t)$ and the constant terms $(1 - 2 + 1 = 0)$:

$$13t = 0 \implies t = 0$$

Step 3: Substitute $t = 0$ back into the line's parametric equations to find the point $(x, y, z)$.

  • $x = 1 + 2(0) = 1$
  • $y = 4(0) = 0$
  • $z = 2 - 3(0) = 2$

Therefore, the line intersects the plane at the point $(1, 0, 2)$.

Question 8: Finding the Line of Intersection Between Two Planes

Problem

Given the planes $\pi_1: \; x + y - z = 2$ and $\pi_2: \; 2x - y + 3z = 1$, find the line of intersection between $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$.

Final Answer

Board Solution (using $2x+y=2$ for $z=0$): $P_1\!\left(\frac{3}{4}, \frac{1}{2}, 0\right), \; P_2\!\left(0, \frac{7}{2}, \frac{3}{2}\right), \; \mathbf{d} = \left\langle -\frac{3}{4}, 3, \frac{3}{2} \right\rangle$
Exact System Solution (using $x+y=2$ for $z=0$): $x = 1 + 2t, \; y = 1 - 5t, \; z = -3t$

Step-by-Step Solution

Method 1: The Two-Point Method (Exact Board Walkthrough)

To find the equation of the line of intersection, it suffices to find two distinct points $P_1$ and $P_2$ that lie on both planes simultaneously, and then compute the direction vector $\mathbf{d} = \overrightarrow{P_1 P_2}$.

Step 1: Find the first point $P_1$ by setting $z = 0$.

  • Note on the board transcription: When setting $z = 0$ on the board, the instructor wrote the system as: $$\begin{cases} 2x + y = 2 \\ 2x - y = 1 \end{cases}$$ Adding the two equations eliminates $y$: $$4x = 3 \implies x = \frac{3}{4}$$ Substituting $x = \frac{3}{4}$ into $2x - y = 1$: $$2\!\left(\frac{3}{4}\right) - y = 1 \implies \frac{3}{2} - 1 = y \implies y = \frac{1}{2}$$ This gives the board's first point: $P_1\!\left(\frac{3}{4}, \frac{1}{2}, 0\right)$.
  • Exact arithmetic for $\pi_1: x + y - z = 2$: Setting $z = 0$ in $x + y - z = 2$ and $2x - y + 3z = 1$ gives: $$\begin{cases} x + y = 2 \\ 2x - y = 1 \end{cases} \implies 3x = 3 \implies x = 1, \; y = 1 \implies P_1(1, 1, 0)$$

Step 2: Find the second point $P_2$ by setting $x = 0$.

Setting $x = 0$ in both $\pi_1$ ($x + y - z = 2$) and $\pi_2$ ($2x - y + 3z = 1$) yields the $2 \times 2$ system in $y$ and $z$:

$$\begin{cases} y - z = 2 \\ -y + 3z = 1 \end{cases}$$

Adding the two equations eliminates $y$:

$$2z = 3 \implies z = \frac{3}{2}$$

Substituting $z = \frac{3}{2}$ into $y - z = 2$:

$$y - \frac{3}{2} = 2 \implies y = 2 + \frac{3}{2} = \frac{7}{2}$$

Thus, the second point on the line of intersection is:

$$P_2\!\left(0, \frac{7}{2}, \frac{3}{2}\right)$$

Step 3: Compute the direction vector $\mathbf{d} = \overrightarrow{P_1 P_2}$ and write the line equation.

  • Using the board's $P_1\!\left(\frac{3}{4}, \frac{1}{2}, 0\right)$ and $P_2\!\left(0, \frac{7}{2}, \frac{3}{2}\right)$: $$\mathbf{d} = \overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} = \left\langle 0 - \frac{3}{4}, \; \frac{7}{2} - \frac{1}{2}, \; \frac{3}{2} - 0 \right\rangle = \left\langle -\frac{3}{4}, \; 3, \; \frac{3}{2} \right\rangle$$ Using $P_1\!\left(\frac{3}{4}, \frac{1}{2}, 0\right)$ and $\mathbf{d}$, the parametric equations are $x = \frac{3}{4} - \frac{3}{4}t, \; y = \frac{1}{2} + 3t, \; z = \frac{3}{2}t$.
  • Using the exact $P_1(1, 1, 0)$ and $P_2\!\left(0, \frac{7}{2}, \frac{3}{2}\right)$ (and verifying via Cross Product): $$\mathbf{d} = \overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} = \left\langle 0 - 1, \; \frac{7}{2} - 1, \; \frac{3}{2} - 0 \right\rangle = \left\langle -1, \; \frac{5}{2}, \; \frac{3}{2} \right\rangle$$ Multiplying by $-2$ gives $\mathbf{d} = \langle 2, -5, -3 \rangle$, which matches the cross product of the plane normals $\mathbf{n}_1 \times \mathbf{n}_2 = \langle 1, 1, -1 \rangle \times \langle 2, -1, 3 \rangle = \langle 2, -5, -3 \rangle$. With $P_1(1, 1, 0)$, the line of intersection is: $$x = 1 + 2t, \quad y = 1 - 5t, \quad z = -3t$$

Problem

Find the angle between the planes $\pi_1: \; x + y + z = 1$ and $\pi_2: \; x + 2y + 2z = 1$.

Final Answer

$$\theta = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{5}{3\sqrt{3}}\right) = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{5\sqrt{3}}{9}\right)$$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Extract the normal vectors $\mathbf{n}_1$ and $\mathbf{n}_2$ of the two planes.

Step 2: Apply the angle between planes formula.

The angle $\theta$ between two planes is the angle between their normal vectors:

$$\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{n}_1 \cdot \mathbf{n}_2}{\|\mathbf{n}_1\| \|\mathbf{n}_2\|}$$
  • Dot product: $\mathbf{n}_1 \cdot \mathbf{n}_2 = (1)(1) + (1)(2) + (1)(2) = 1 + 2 + 2 = 5$
  • Magnitude of $\mathbf{n}_1$: $\|\mathbf{n}_1\| = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{3}$
  • Magnitude of $\mathbf{n}_2$: $\|\mathbf{n}_2\| = \sqrt{1^2 + 2^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{9} = 3$

Step 3: Solve for $\theta$.

$$\cos\theta = \frac{5}{3\sqrt{3}} \implies \theta = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{5}{3\sqrt{3}}\right)$$

1. Distance Between Two Points & Distance from a Point to a Line in 3D Space

In three-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^3$, we frequently need to compute the shortest (perpendicular) distance between geometric objects such as points, lines, planes, and spheres.

1.1 Distance Between Two Points

Given two points $A(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $B(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ in 3D space, the Euclidean distance $\overline{AB}$ between them is given by:

$$\overline{AB} = \sqrt{(\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2 + (\Delta z)^2} = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2}$$

1.2 Distance from a Point $P_0$ to a Line $L$ in 3D Space

Suppose we are given a line $L$ in 3D space and an external point $P_0(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ not on the line.

To find the perpendicular distance $h$ (also denoted $D$) from $P_0$ to the line $L$:

  1. Extract any point $P_1(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ lying on the line $L$ (for example, by setting the parameter $t = 0$ in the parametric equations of $L$).
  2. Extract the direction vector $\vec{d} = \langle a, b, c \rangle$ (often denoted $\mathbf{v}$) of the line $L$ from the coefficients of the parameter $t$.
  3. Form the connecting vector $\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0} = P_0 - P_1 = \langle x_0 - x_1, \, y_0 - y_1, \, z_0 - z_1 \rangle$ from the point on the line to the external point.

Geometric Derivation via the Cross Product: Let $\theta$ be the angle between the vector $\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0}$ and the line's direction vector $\vec{d}$.

Dropping a perpendicular of length $h$ from $P_0$ to the line $L$ forms a right triangle with hypotenuse $\|\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0}\|$, so:

$$h = \|\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0}\| \sin\theta$$

Recall from the geometric definition of the cross product that $\|\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0} \times \vec{d}\| = \|\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0}\| \|\vec{d}\| \sin\theta$. Dividing both sides by $\|\vec{d}\|$ yields the Point-to-Line Distance Formula:

$$h = D = \frac{\|\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0} \times \vec{d}\|}{\|\vec{d}\|} = \frac{\|\overrightarrow{P_0 P_1} \times \mathbf{v}\|}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}$$
Example

Question 1: Distance Between a Line and a Point in 3D Space

Problem

Find the distance between the line

$$L: \quad x = 2t, \quad y = 1 - t, \quad z = 2 + 3t$$

and the point $P_0(-1, 2, 3)$.

Final Answer

$$h = \frac{\sqrt{42}}{\sqrt{14}} = \sqrt{3}$$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Identify a point $P_1$ on the line and the direction vector $\vec{d}$.

From the parametric equations of the line $L$:

$$x = 0 + 2t, \quad y = 1 - 1t, \quad z = 2 + 3t$$
  • Setting $t = 0$ gives a point on the line: $P_1(0, 1, 2)$.
  • Reading the coefficients of $t$ gives the direction vector of the line: $\vec{d} = \langle 2, -1, 3 \rangle$.

Step 2: Construct the vector $\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0}$ from the line to the point.

Subtract the coordinates of $P_1(0, 1, 2)$ from $P_0(-1, 2, 3)$:

$$\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0} = P_0 - P_1 = \langle -1 - 0, \, 2 - 1, \, 3 - 2 \rangle = \langle -1, 1, 1 \rangle$$

Step 3: Compute the cross product $\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0} \times \vec{d}$.

Set up the $3 \times 3$ determinant with $\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0} = \langle -1, 1, 1 \rangle$ and $\vec{d} = \langle 2, -1, 3 \rangle$:

$$\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0} \times \vec{d} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ -1 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 & 3 \end{vmatrix}$$

Expand along the first row (remembering the alternating signs $+, -, +$):

  • $\mathbf{i}$-component: $(1)(3) - (1)(-1) = 3 + 1 = 4$
  • $\mathbf{j}$-component: $-\big[(-1)(3) - (1)(2)\big] = -(-3 - 2) = +5$
  • $\mathbf{k}$-component: $(-1)(-1) - (1)(2) = 1 - 2 = -1$

Thus, the cross product vector is:

$$\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0} \times \vec{d} = \langle 4, 5, -1 \rangle$$

Step 4: Compute the magnitudes and apply the distance formula.

Find the magnitude of the cross product $\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0} \times \vec{d}$:

$$\|\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0} \times \vec{d}\| = \sqrt{4^2 + 5^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{16 + 25 + 1} = \sqrt{42}$$

Find the magnitude of the direction vector $\vec{d} = \langle 2, -1, 3 \rangle$:

$$\|\vec{d}\| = \sqrt{2^2 + (-1)^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 9} = \sqrt{14}$$

Divide the two magnitudes to obtain the perpendicular distance $h$:

$$h = \frac{\|\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0} \times \vec{d}\|}{\|\vec{d}\|} = \frac{\sqrt{42}}{\sqrt{14}} = \sqrt{\frac{42}{14}} = \sqrt{3}$$

2. Distance from a Point to a Plane

Suppose we want to find the perpendicular distance $D$ from a point $P_0(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ (or $P_1(x_1, y_1, z_1)$) to a plane $\pi$ with equation:

$$ax + by + cz + d = 0$$

where $\vec{n} = \langle a, b, c \rangle$ is the normal vector to the plane.

Vector Projection Derivation

Let $P_1(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ be any point on the plane $\pi$, so that $ax_1 + by_1 + cz_1 + d = 0$, or equivalently $d = -(ax_1 + by_1 + cz_1)$.

The shortest distance $D$ from $P_0(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ to the plane is the absolute value of the scalar projection (component) of the vector $\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0} = \langle x_0 - x_1, \, y_0 - y_1, \, z_0 - z_1 \rangle$ onto the normal vector $\vec{n} = \langle a, b, c \rangle$:

$$D = |\operatorname{comp}_{\vec{n}} \overrightarrow{P_1 P_0}| = \frac{|\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0} \cdot \vec{n}|}{\|\vec{n}\|} = \frac{|a(x_0 - x_1) + b(y_0 - y_1) + c(z_0 - z_1)|}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}$$

Expanding the numerator and substituting $d = -(ax_1 + by_1 + cz_1)$ gives the standard Point-to-Plane Distance Formula:

$$D = \frac{|ax_0 + by_0 + cz_0 + d|}{\|\vec{n}\|} = \frac{|ax_0 + by_0 + cz_0 + d|}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}$$

Crucial Caution: Before applying this formula, you must move all terms of the plane equation to one side so that the other side is zero ($ax + by + cz + d = 0$).

Also note that if the plane is already written in point-normal form $a(x - x_1) + b(y - y_1) + c(z - z_1) = 0$, you can directly substitute $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ into the left-hand expression inside the absolute value without even expanding it!

Example

Question 2: Distance Between a Point and a Plane

Problem

Find the distance between the point $P_0(1, -2, 4)$ and the plane $3x + 2y + 6z = 5$.

Final Answer

$$D = \frac{18}{7}$$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Rewrite the plane equation in general form $ax + by + cz + d = 0$.

Subtract $5$ from both sides so that the right-hand side is zero:

$$3x + 2y + 6z - 5 = 0$$

From this equation, the normal vector is $\vec{n} = \langle 3, 2, 6 \rangle$, so $a = 3$, $b = 2$, $c = 6$, and $d = -5$.

Step 2: Substitute the point $P_0(1, -2, 4)$ and normal vector into the distance formula.

$$D = \frac{|ax_0 + by_0 + cz_0 + d|}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} = \frac{|3(1) + 2(-2) + 6(4) - 5|}{\sqrt{3^2 + 2^2 + 6^2}}$$

Step 3: Simplify the numerator and denominator.

$$D = \frac{|3 - 4 + 24 - 5|}{\sqrt{9 + 4 + 36}} = \frac{|18|}{\sqrt{49}} = \frac{18}{7}$$

3. Distances Between Lines and Planes: Parallel Lines, Skew Lines, Parallel Planes & Line Parallel to a Plane

Rather than memorizing four separate new formulas, every distance problem between two lines, two planes, or a line and a plane reduces to one of the two fundamental formulas we already know:

Point-to-Line or Point-to-Plane.

  1. Distance Between Two Parallel Lines ($L_1 \parallel L_2$):

    Since the distance between two parallel lines is constant everywhere along the lines, simply pick any point $P_0$ on the first line $L_1$, and compute the Point-to-Line distance from $P_0$ to the second line $L_2$ using $h = \frac{\|\overrightarrow{P_1 P_0} \times \vec{d}\|}{\|\vec{d}\|}$.

  2. Distance Between Two Skew Lines ($L_1$ and $L_2$ neither parallel nor intersecting):

    Two skew lines always lie in two unique parallel planes. Thus, the distance between $L_1$ and $L_2$ reduces to a Point-to-Plane problem:

    • First, verify the lines are not parallel by checking that their direction vectors $\vec{d}_1$ and $\vec{d}_2$ are not scalar multiples of each other.
    • Construct a plane $\pi$ that contains $L_2$ and is parallel to $L_1$. Because $\pi$ is parallel to both lines, its normal vector $\vec{n}$ is perpendicular to both $\vec{d}_1$ and $\vec{d}_2$:$$\vec{n} = \vec{d}_1 \times \vec{d}_2$$
    • Pick a point $P_2$ on $L_2$ and write the point-normal equation of the plane $\pi$ using $P_2$ and $\vec{n}$.
    • Pick a point $P_1$ on $L_1$ and compute the Point-to-Plane distance $D(P_1, \pi)$ from $P_1$ to the plane $\pi$.
    • Equivalent Direct Vector Formula: Substituting $P_1$ into the plane equation is algebraically identical to projecting $\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2}$ onto $\vec{n} = \vec{d}_1 \times \vec{d}_2$:$$D = |\operatorname{comp}_{\vec{n}} \overrightarrow{P_1 P_2}| = \frac{|\overrightarrow{P_1 P_2} \cdot (\vec{d}_1 \times \vec{d}_2)|}{\|\vec{d}_1 \times \vec{d}_2\|}$$
  3. Distance Between Two Parallel Planes ($\pi_1 \parallel \pi_2$):

    Pick any point on the first plane $\pi_1$ (e.g., by setting two variables such as $x = 0, y = 0$ and solving for $z$), and compute its Point-to-Plane distance to the second plane $\pi_2$.

  4. Distance Between a Line Parallel to a Plane ($L \parallel \pi$):

    Pick any point on the line $L$ (e.g., at $t = 0$) and compute its Point-to-Plane distance to the plane $\pi$.

$$\vec{n} = \vec{d}_1 \times \vec{d}_2 = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ 1 & 3 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 & 4 \end{vmatrix}$$

  • $\mathbf{j}$-component: $-\big[(1)(4) - (-1)(2)\big] = -(4 + 2) = -6$

$$13(x - 0) - 6(y - 3) - 5(z + 3) = 0$$

Setting $t = 0$ in the equations for $L_1$ gives the point $P_1(1, -2, 4)$.

Substitute $P_1(1, -2, 4)$ into the Point-to-Plane distance formula:

4. Distance Between a Sphere and a Plane & Sphere-Plane Tangency

Consider a sphere in 3D space with standard equation:

$$(x - x_0)^2 + (y - y_0)^2 + (z - z_0)^2 = r^2$$

which has center $C(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ and radius $r$, together with a plane $\pi: ax + by + cz + d = 0$.

1. Shortest Distance Between a Sphere and a Plane

Let $d(C, \pi)$ denote the perpendicular distance from the sphere's center $C(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ to the plane $\pi$:

$$d(C, \pi) = \frac{|ax_0 + by_0 + cz_0 + d|}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}$$

When $d(C, \pi) > r$ (so the plane lies outside the sphere without intersecting it), the shortest distance from the surface of the sphere to the plane is obtained by subtracting the radius $r$ along the perpendicular line of sight:

$$d(\text{sphere}, \text{plane}) = d(C, \pi) - r$$

(Remark: If a problem asks for the farthest / maximum distance from a point on the sphere to the plane, you add the radius instead: $d_{\max} = d(C, \pi) + r$.)

2. Tangency Condition (When a Plane Touches a Sphere)

A plane $\pi$ touches (is tangent to) a sphere at a single point if and only if the perpendicular distance from the center $C$ of the sphere to the plane is exactly equal to the sphere's radius $r$:

$$\text{Plane touches sphere} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad d(C, \pi) = r$$
Example

Question 5: Distance Between a Sphere and a Plane

Problem

Find the distance between the sphere

$$x^2 + (y - 2)^2 + (z + 3)^2 = 25$$

and the plane $3x + 2y - z + 20 = 0$.

Final Answer

$$d(\text{sphere}, \text{plane}) = \frac{27}{\sqrt{14}} - 5$$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Find the center $C$ and radius $r$ of the sphere.

Comparing $x^2 + (y - 2)^2 + (z + 3)^2 = 25$ with $(x - x_0)^2 + (y - y_0)^2 + (z - z_0)^2 = r^2$:

  • Center of the sphere: $C(0, 2, -3)$
  • Radius of the sphere: $r = \sqrt{25} = 5$

Step 2: Compute the distance $d(C, \pi)$ from the center $C(0, 2, -3)$ to the plane $\pi: 3x + 2y - z + 20 = 0$.

Using the Point-to-Plane distance formula with $\vec{n} = \langle 3, 2, -1 \rangle$:

$$d(C, \pi) = \frac{|3(0) + 2(2) - (-3) + 20|}{\sqrt{3^2 + 2^2 + (-1)^2}} = \frac{|0 + 4 + 3 + 20|}{\sqrt{9 + 4 + 1}} = \frac{27}{\sqrt{14}}$$

Step 3: Subtract the radius $r$ from $d(C, \pi)$.

Since the distance from the center to the plane is $\frac{27}{\sqrt{14}} \approx 7.22 > 5$, the shortest distance between the surface of the sphere and the plane is:

$$d(\text{sphere}, \text{plane}) = d(C, \pi) - r = \frac{27}{\sqrt{14}} - 5$$

Jump to idea:
Explanation

1. Cylinders in 3D Space (Circular, Elliptic & Parabolic Cylinders)

In two-dimensional space ($\mathbb{R}^2$), an equation involving two variables such as $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$ or $y = x^2$ represents a planar curve (a circle or a parabola).

However, in three-dimensional space ($\mathbb{R}^3$), whenever one of the three coordinate variables ($x$, $y$, or $z$) is completely absent from the equation, that missing variable is unrestricted (free to take any real value $(-\infty, \infty)$).

Geometrically, the 2D curve in the plane of the two present variables is swept infinitely along lines parallel to the axis of the missing variable, generating a surface called a cylinder.

The Missing-Variable Rule for Cylinders in $\mathbb{R}^3$

  • Missing $z$ (equation only contains $x$ and $y$): The cylinder extends infinitely in the $z$-direction (parallel to the $z$-axis).
  • Missing $y$ (equation only contains $x$ and $z$): The cylinder extends infinitely in the $y$-direction (parallel to the $y$-axis).
  • Missing $x$ (equation only contains $y$ and $z$): The cylinder extends infinitely in the $x$-direction (parallel to the $x$-axis).

A. Circular Cylinder

A circular cylinder occurs when two variables are squared with the same positive coefficient (or equal denominators in standard form) and the third variable is missing:

$$x^2 + y^2 = r^2 \quad (\text{axis along } z), \qquad x^2 + z^2 = r^2 \quad (\text{axis along } y), \qquad y^2 + z^2 = r^2 \quad (\text{axis along } x)$$

Each cross-section (trace) perpendicular to the missing variable's axis is a circle of radius $r$ centered at the origin $(0,0)$ in that plane.

B. Elliptic Cylinder

An elliptic cylinder occurs when two variables are squared and added together, equal to $1$, with unequal denominators ($a^2 \neq b^2$), while the third variable is missing:

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \quad (\text{along } z\text{-axis}), \qquad \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{z^2}{b^2} = 1 \quad (\text{along } y\text{-axis}), \qquad \frac{y^2}{a^2} + \frac{z^2}{b^2} = 1 \quad (\text{along } x\text{-axis})$$

For $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$, the trace in the $xy$-plane ($z=0$) is an ellipse intersecting the $x$-axis at $(\pm a, 0)$ and the $y$-axis at $(0, \pm b)$, extended vertically along the $z$-axis.

C. Parabolic Cylinder

A parabolic cylinder occurs when only two variables appear in the equation, where one variable is squared (degree 2) and the other variable is linear (degree 1), while the third variable is missing:

$$y = x^2, \qquad z = x^2, \qquad z = y^2, \quad \text{etc.}$$

For example, to sketch $y = x^2$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$:

  1. First sketch the parabola $y = x^2$ in the 2D $xy$-plane (opening toward the positive $y$-axis with vertex at $(0,0)$).
  2. Since the variable $z$ is missing, translate copies of this parabola vertically parallel to the $z$-axis and connect their edges with vertical rulings to form a curved trough/sheet extending in the $z$-direction.
Example

  • Because the denominators under $x^2$ and $y^2$ are different ($a^2 = 1 \neq b^2 = \frac{1}{4}$), the trace in the $xy$-plane is an ellipse rather than a circle.

Step 3: Find the intercepts to sketch the surface.

  • Along the $x$-axis ($y=0$): $x^2 = 1 \implies x = \pm 1$ (since $a = \sqrt{1} = 1$).
  • Along the $y$-axis ($x=0$): $\frac{y^2}{1/4} = 1 \implies y^2 = \frac{1}{4} \implies y = \pm \frac{1}{2}$ (since $b = \sqrt{\frac{1}{4}} = \frac{1}{2}$).

Plotting the ellipse through $(\pm 1, 0)$ and $(0, \pm \frac{1}{2})$ in the $xy$-plane and extending it parallel to the $z$-axis produces an elliptic cylinder in the $z$-direction.

2. Spheres & Ellipsoids (All Three Variables Squared & Positive)

When all three variables ($x$, $y$, and $z$) are present, squared, separated by plus signs ($+, +, +$), and equal to a positive constant, the surface is a closed, bounded oval shape: either a sphere or an ellipsoid.

2. Sphere

A sphere centered at the origin $(0,0,0)$ with radius $r$ has the equation:

$$x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = r^2 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \frac{x^2}{r^2} + \frac{y^2}{r^2} + \frac{z^2}{r^2} = 1$$

For example, $\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{9} + \frac{z^2}{9} = 1$ (or $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 9$) is a sphere because all three denominators are equal ($a^2 = b^2 = c^2 = 9$).

3. Ellipsoid

An ellipsoid centered at $(0,0,0)$ has the standard equation:

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1$$

where $a, b, c > 0$ are the semi-axes along the $x$-, $y$-, and $z$-directions, respectively, and the denominators $a^2, b^2, c^2$ are not all equal (looks like a 3D rugby ball or stretched sphere).

Traces & Intercepts of an Ellipsoid $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1$

  • $x$-intercepts ($y=z=0$): $(\pm a, 0, 0)$
  • $y$-intercepts ($x=z=0$): $(0, \pm b, 0)$
  • $z$-intercepts ($x=y=0$): $(0, 0, \pm c)$
  • Coordinate Traces: Setting $x=0$, $y=0$, or $z=0$ always yields an ellipse in the corresponding coordinate plane. If the equation is shifted to $\frac{(x-x_0)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y-y_0)^2}{b^2} + \frac{(z-z_0)^2}{c^2} = 1$, the ellipsoid is centered at $C(x_0, y_0, z_0)$.

3. Hyperboloids of One Sheet & Two Sheets

When all three variables $x, y, z$ are squared and the right-hand side is normalized to $+1$, the number of minus signs on the left-hand side determines whether we have a Hyperboloid of One Sheet (one minus sign) or a Hyperboloid of Two Sheets (two minus signs).

4. Hyperboloid of One Sheet (One Minus Sign when RHS $= +1$)

Standard equation (with axis along the $z$-axis):

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} - \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1$$
  • Sign Pattern: Two positive squared terms and one negative squared term ($+, +, -$) equal to $+1$.
  • Axis of Symmetry Rule: The surface opens around the axis corresponding to the variable with the odd (negative) sign:
    • $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} - \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1 \implies$ negative sign on $z^2 \implies$ opens along the $z$-axis.
    • $\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1 \implies$ negative sign on $y^2 \implies$ opens along the $y$-axis.
    • $-\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1 \implies$ negative sign on $x^2 \implies$ opens along the $x$-axis.
  • Geometric Shape & Sketching: It forms a single, continuous hourglass or nuclear cooling-tower shape surrounding its axis. In $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} - \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1$, setting $z = 0$ gives the central "waist" ellipse $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$, and horizontal cross-sections above and below ($z = \pm k$) are larger ellipses connected by hyperbolic side curves.

5. Hyperboloid of Two Sheets (Two Minus Signs when RHS $= +1$)

Standard equation (with axis along the $z$-axis):

$$\frac{z^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} - \frac{y^2}{c^2} = 1$$
  • Sign Pattern: One positive squared term and two negative squared terms ($+, -, -$) equal to $+1$.
  • Axis of Symmetry Rule: The two separate bowls open along the axis corresponding to the variable with the single positive sign:
    • $\frac{z^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} - \frac{y^2}{c^2} = 1 \implies$ positive sign on $z^2 \implies$ two bowls along the $z$-axis with vertices at $(0, 0, \pm a)$ (found by setting $x=y=0 \implies \frac{z^2}{a^2}=1 \implies z = \pm a$).
    • $\frac{y^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} - \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1 \implies$ positive sign on $y^2 \implies$ two bowls along the $y$-axis with vertices at $(0, \pm a, 0)$.
    • $\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} - \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1 \implies$ positive sign on $x^2 \implies$ two bowls along the $x$-axis with vertices at $(\pm a, 0, 0)$.
  • Why Two Separate Sheets? In $\frac{z^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} - \frac{y^2}{c^2} = 1$, if we set $z = 0$, we get $-\frac{x^2}{b^2} - \frac{y^2}{c^2} = 1$, which is impossible for real $x, y$ (a negative number cannot equal $+1$). Thus, the surface does not intersect the $xy$-plane at all; it is split into an upper bowl ($z \ge a$) and a lower bowl ($z \le -a$).

Identify the surface, its center, and its axis of orientation:

Final Answer

Hyperboloid of One Sheet centered at $C(1, -2, 2)$, oriented parallel to the $z$-direction.

For a hyperboloid of one sheet, the axis of symmetry follows the variable with the negative sign.

Problem Q5: Normalizing & Classifying a Quadric Surface

Problem

Identify the quadric surface and its axis of orientation:

$$\frac{x^2}{3} + \frac{z^2}{4} - y^2 = 5$$
Final Answer

Hyperboloid of One Sheet ($\frac{x^2}{15} + \frac{z^2}{20} - \frac{y^2}{5} = 1$) oriented along the $y$-direction.

In a hyperboloid of one sheet, the axis of symmetry corresponds to the variable with the negative sign.

4. Double Cones, Elliptic Paraboloids & Hyperbolic Paraboloids (Saddles)

6. Double Cone (Elliptic / Circular Cone)

Standard equation (with axis along the $z$-axis):

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} - \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \frac{z^2}{c^2} = \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2}$$
  • How to Distinguish a Cone from a Hyperboloid of One Sheet: Both have all three variables squared with two positive terms and one negative term ($+, +, -$), EXCEPT the right-hand side of a cone is $0$ instead of $1$!
  • Axis Rule: The axis of the cone is along the variable with the different (negative) sign when set equal to $0$ (or the isolated squared variable on one side of $\frac{z^2}{c^2} = \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2}$).
  • Important Special Case ($z^2 = x^2 + y^2$) & Half-Cones:
    • The full equation $z^2 = x^2 + y^2$ gives a double circular cone meeting at the vertex $(0,0,0)$.
    • Taking the square root of both sides gives $z = \pm\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$:
      • $z = +\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ represents only the upper half-cone ($z \ge 0$).
      • $z = -\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ represents only the lower half-cone ($z \le 0$).

7. Elliptic (or Circular) Paraboloid

Standard equation (with axis along the $z$-axis):

$$\frac{z}{c} = \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2}$$
  • Identification Rule: All three variables ($x, y, z$) are present, two variables are squared with the SAME sign, and one variable is linear (degree 1). (Contrast this with a parabolic cylinder like $z = x^2$, where the third variable $y$ is completely missing!)
  • Axis & Opening Direction: The bowl opens along the axis of the linear (degree-1) variable:
    • If $z = \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2}$ ($c > 0$): Bowl opens upward along the $+z$-axis with vertex at $(0,0,0)$.
    • If $z = -\left(\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2}\right)$ ($c < 0$): Bowl opens downward along the $-z$-axis.
    • Similarly, $x = y^2 + z^2$ opens along the $+x$-axis, and $y = x^2 + z^2$ opens along the $+y$-axis.
  • At height $z = c$, the cross-section is the ellipse $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ with semi-axes $a$ (along $x$) and $b$ (along $y$). When $a = b$, the cross-sections are circles, and it is often simply called a circular paraboloid (or just paraboloid).

8. Hyperbolic Paraboloid (Saddle Surface)

Standard equation:

$$\frac{z}{c} = \frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2}$$
  • Identification Rule: All three variables ($x, y, z$) are present, one variable is linear (degree 1), and the two squared variables have OPPOSITE signs (one positive, one negative).
  • Exam Note: In Math 201, you are required to identify the hyperbolic paraboloid from its equation, but you are not asked to draw its 3D saddle graph by hand (whereas you must know how to sketch cylinders, spheres, ellipsoids, hyperboloids, cones, and elliptic/circular paraboloids).

5. Fast Quadric Surface Identification Rules (Cheat Sheet)

Anas Abu Zahra's Rapid Quadric Surface Identification Algorithm

  1. Count how many variables ($x, y, z$) appear in the equation:
    • Only 2 variables present (1 variable missing) $\implies$ Cylinder extending along the missing variable's axis:
      • Both squared, same sign, equal denominators ($x^2 + y^2 = r^2$) $\implies$ Circular Cylinder.
      • Both squared, same sign, unequal denominators ($\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$) $\implies$ Elliptic Cylinder.
      • One squared, one linear ($y = x^2$ or $z = 1 - x^2$) $\implies$ Parabolic Cylinder.
  2. All 3 variables ($x, y, z$) appear, and ALL THREE are squared ($x^2, y^2, z^2$):
    • Check the constant term (move all variable terms to the LHS and constant to the RHS):
      • RHS $= 0$ (with 2 positive, 1 negative term, e.g., $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} - \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 0$) $\implies$ Double Cone (if given with a square root like $z = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}$, it is a Half-Cone).
      • RHS $= +1$ (after dividing by the constant):
        • All 3 positive ($+, +, +$): Equal denominators $\implies$ Sphere; unequal denominators $\implies$ Ellipsoid.
        • 2 positive, 1 negative ($+, +, -$) $\implies$ Hyperboloid of One Sheet (axis along the negative variable).
        • 1 positive, 2 negative ($+, -, -$) $\implies$ Hyperboloid of Two Sheets (axis along the positive variable).
  3. All 3 variables ($x, y, z$) appear, with TWO squared and ONE linear (degree 1):
    • Isolate the linear variable on one side (e.g., $z - z_0 = \dots$):
      • Two squared terms have the SAME sign ($z = \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2}$) $\implies$ Paraboloid (Elliptic/Circular Paraboloid) opening along the linear variable.
      • Two squared terms have OPPOSITE signs ($z = \frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2}$) $\implies$ Hyperbolic Paraboloid.

1. Shifted Quadric Surfaces & Completing the Square

In Part 1 of Section 12.6 (Syllabus 11.6), we studied quadric surfaces centered at the origin $(0,0,0)$.

In general, a second-degree equation in three variables $x, y, z$ has the form:

$$Ax^2 + By^2 + Cz^2 + Dx + Ey + Fz + G = 0$$

When linear terms ($Dx, Ey, Fz$) are present alongside quadratic terms ($Ax^2, By^2, Cz^2$), the quadric surface is shifted (translated) so that its center or vertex is located at a point $C(h, k, l)$ instead of the origin $(0,0,0)$.

How to Identify a Shifted Quadric Surface

  1. Group terms by variable: Collect the $x$-terms $(Ax^2 + Dx)$, $y$-terms $(By^2 + Ey)$, and $z$-terms $(Cz^2 + Fz)$ together.
  2. Factor out leading coefficients: Before completing the square on any variable, make sure the coefficient of the squared term inside the parentheses is $+1$. For example, if you have $-y^2 + 2y$, factor out $-1$ to get $-(y^2 - 2y)$.
  3. Complete the square: For each expression of the form $u^2 + bu$, add and subtract $\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2$:$$u^2 + bu = \left(u + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2$$Be careful to multiply the subtracted constant by any factored-out coefficient when moving it outside the parentheses.
  4. Write in Standard Form: Move the remaining constant to the right-hand side (RHS) and divide through so the RHS equals $1$ (for ellipsoids and hyperboloids) or $0$ (for cones). Read the center/vertex $C(h, k, l)$ from the shifted terms $(x - h)$, $(y - k)$, and $(z - l)$.

Question 8: Identifying a Quadric Surface by Completing the Square

Problem

Identify the surface given by the equation:

$$x^2 - y^2 + z^2 + 2y - 2x + 4z + 2 = 0$$
Final Answer

Hyperboloid of one sheet with center $C(1, 1, -2)$, opening along the $y$-direction (parallel to the $y$-axis), with standard form:

$$\frac{(x - 1)^2}{2} - \frac{(y - 1)^2}{2} + \frac{(z + 2)^2}{2} = 1$$
Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Group terms by variable ($x$, $y$, and $z$).

Rearrange the equation so that terms with the same variable are adjacent.

For the $y$-terms ($-y^2 + 2y$), factor out a negative sign so that the coefficient of $y^2$ inside the parentheses is $+1$:

$$(x^2 - 2x) - (y^2 - 2y) + (z^2 + 4z) + 2 = 0$$

Step 2: Complete the square for each variable.

  • For $x^2 - 2x$: Half of $-2$ is $-1$, and $(-1)^2 = 1$. Add and subtract $1$:$$x^2 - 2x + 1 - 1 = (x - 1)^2 - 1$$
  • For $-(y^2 - 2y)$: Half of $-2$ is $-1$, and $(-1)^2 = 1$. Add and subtract $1$ inside the parentheses:$$-(y^2 - 2y + 1 - 1) = -\left((y - 1)^2 - 1\right)$$
  • For $z^2 + 4z$: Half of $4$ is $2$, and $2^2 = 4$. Add and subtract $4$:$$z^2 + 4z + 4 - 4 = (z + 2)^2 - 4$$

Substituting these back into the equation gives:

$$(x^2 - 2x + 1) - 1 - (y^2 - 2y + 1 - 1) + (z^2 + 4z + 4) - 4 + 2 = 0$$$$(x - 1)^2 - 1 - \left((y - 1)^2 - 1\right) + (z + 2)^2 - 2 = 0$$

Step 3: Distribute the negative sign and simplify constants.

Distributing the minus sign across $-\left((y - 1)^2 - 1\right)$ yields $-(y - 1)^2 + 1$. Notice that $-1$ and $+1$ cancel out:

$$(x - 1)^2 - 1 - (y - 1)^2 + 1 + (z + 2)^2 - 2 = 0$$$$(x - 1)^2 - (y - 1)^2 + (z + 2)^2 = 2$$

Step 4: Divide by $2$ to obtain the standard form.

To put the quadric surface in standard form, the constant on the right-hand side must be $1$. Dividing every term by $2$:

$$\frac{(x - 1)^2}{2} - \frac{(y - 1)^2}{2} + \frac{(z + 2)^2}{2} = 1$$

Step 5: Identify the surface, center, and axis orientation.

  • All three variables are squared and the right-hand side equals $1$.
  • There are two positive terms and one negative term ($-\frac{(y-1)^2}{2}$), which is the signature of a Hyperboloid of One Sheet.
  • Setting each squared group to zero gives the shifted center: $x - 1 = 0 \implies x = 1$, $y - 1 = 0 \implies y = 1$, and $z + 2 = 0 \implies z = -2$. Thus, the center is $C(1, 1, -2)$.
  • Because the single negative sign belongs to the $y$-term, the axis of symmetry is parallel to the $y$-axis ($y$-direction).
Explanation

2. Radical Equations & Half-Quadric Surfaces

When an equation involves a principal square root $\sqrt{\cdots}$, squaring both sides produces a standard quadric surface equation, but we must preserve the sign restriction imposed by the square root:

  • Since $\sqrt{A} \ge 0$ by definition, an equation of the form $z - l = +\sqrt{A}$ restricts the surface to the upper half where $z \ge l$.
  • An equation of the form $z - l = -\sqrt{A}$ restricts the surface to the lower half where $z \le l$.

For a cone $z^2 = x^2 + y^2$, the full surface consists of two nappes (upper and lower cones meeting at the vertex).

Taking $z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ isolates the upper cone ($z \ge 0$), whereas $z = -\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ isolates the lower cone ($z \le 0$).

Example

Question 9: Identifying a Radical Surface in 3D

Problem

Identify the surface given by the equation:

$$z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$$
Final Answer

Upper half of a circular cone (upper cone) with vertex at the origin $(0, 0, 0)$, opening upward along the positive $z$-direction ($z \ge 0$).

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Note the sign restriction from the square root.

Because the principal square root is always non-negative ($\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \ge 0$), we immediately have the restriction:

$$z \ge 0$$

Step 2: Square both sides to recognize the parent quadric surface.

$$z^2 = x^2 + y^2 \implies x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 0$$

Step 3: Identify the surface and apply the restriction.

  • The equation $x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 0$ is a circular cone centered at $(0, 0, 0)$ opening along the $z$-direction.
  • Because $z \ge 0$, only the portion above the $xy$-plane exists. Therefore, the surface is the upper circular cone (half-cone in the positive $z$-direction) with vertex at $(0, 0, 0)$.

3. Deriving Quadric Surfaces from Geometric Distance Conditions

A classic exam question format defines a surface as the locus of all points $P(x, y, z)$ satisfying a relationship between distances to given points or planes.

To solve these problems:

  1. Distance between two points $P(x, y, z)$ and $P_1(x_1, y_1, z_1)$:$$d(P, P_1) = \sqrt{(x - x_1)^2 + (y - y_1)^2 + (z - z_1)^2}$$
  2. Distance from a point $P(x, y, z)$ to a plane $\pi: ax + by + cz + d = 0$ with normal vector $\vec{n} = \langle a, b, c \rangle$:$$d(P, \pi) = \frac{|ax + by + cz + d|}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}$$
  3. Square both sides to remove the square root and absolute value, then simplify into standard quadric form.
Example

Question 12: Locus of Points Equating Point Distance and Plane Distance

Problem

A surface consists of all points $P(x, y, z)$ such that the distance from $P$ to the point $(0, 1, 0)$ is twice the distance from $P$ to the plane $y = 1$. Identify this surface.

Final Answer

Circular Cone with vertex (center) at $C(0, 1, 0)$ opening along the $y$-direction, with equation:

$$x^2 - 3(y - 1)^2 + z^2 = 0$$
Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Translate the word problem into a distance equation.

Let $P(x, y, z)$ be any point on the surface, $P_1(0, 1, 0)$ be the given point, and $\pi$ be the plane $y = 1$. The problem states:

$$d(P, P_1) = 2 \, d(P, \pi)$$

Step 2: Write explicit formulas for both distances.

  • The distance from $P(x, y, z)$ to $P_1(0, 1, 0)$ is:$$d(P, P_1) = \sqrt{(x - 0)^2 + (y - 1)^2 + (z - 0)^2} = \sqrt{x^2 + (y - 1)^2 + z^2}$$
  • Write the plane $y = 1$ in general form $ax + by + cz + d = 0$:$$0x + 1y + 0z - 1 = 0 \quad \text{with normal vector } \vec{n} = \langle 0, 1, 0 \rangle$$Using the point-to-plane distance formula:$$d(P, \pi) = \frac{|0x + 1y + 0z - 1|}{\sqrt{0^2 + 1^2 + 0^2}} = \frac{|y - 1|}{1} = |y - 1|$$

Substituting both expressions into $d(P, P_1) = 2 \, d(P, \pi)$ gives:

$$\sqrt{x^2 + (y - 1)^2 + z^2} = 2 \frac{|y - 1|}{1}$$

Step 3: Square both sides and simplify into standard form.

Squaring both sides eliminates the radical on the left and the absolute value on the right:

$$x^2 + (y - 1)^2 + z^2 = 4(y - 1)^2$$

Note: Make sure not to expand $(y - 1)^2$, because it is already factored in shifted form! Simply subtract $4(y - 1)^2$ from both sides:

$$x^2 + (y - 1)^2 - 4(y - 1)^2 + z^2 = 0$$$$x^2 - 3(y - 1)^2 + z^2 = 0$$

Step 4: Identify the surface.

  • All three variables are squared, the right-hand side is $0$, and there are two positive terms ($x^2, z^2$) and one negative term ($-3(y - 1)^2$).
  • This is a Circular Cone with vertex/center at $C(0, 1, 0)$ opening along the $y$-direction (parallel to the $y$-axis).
Explanation

4. Geometric Regions Defined by Inequalities in 2D and 3D

When an equation $f(x, y, z) = c$ is replaced by an inequality ($\le, <, \ge, >$), it no longer represents just a thin surface shell—instead, it describes a region in 2D ($\mathbb{R}^2$) or a solid volume in 3D ($\mathbb{R}^3$).

Key Rules for Interpreting Inequalities

  • 2D vs. 3D Context: An inequality involving only $x$ and $y$, such as $x^2 + y^2 \le r^2$, describes a disk (inside or on a circle) in $\mathbb{R}^2$, but in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (where $z$ is unrestricted) it describes the solid region inside or on an infinite circular cylinder along the $z$-axis.
  • Non-Strict ($\le, \ge$) vs. Strict ($<, >$) Inequalities:
    • $\le$ or $\ge$: The boundary curve/surface is included (drawn with a solid line). We say "on or inside" / "on or outside".
    • $<$ or $>$: The boundary curve/surface is excluded (drawn with a dashed line). We say strictly "inside" or "outside".
  • Compound Inequalities ($A \le f(x,y,z) \le B$): Represents the region sandwiched between the two boundary surfaces $f(x,y,z) = A$ and $f(x,y,z) = B$.
Example

Inequalities Question 1: Region Defined by x² + y² ≤ 4

Problem

Sketch and identify the region described by the inequality (in both 2D and 3D):

$$x^2 + y^2 \le 4$$
Final Answer
  • In 2D ($\mathbb{R}^2$): The region on or inside the circle centered at $C(0, 0)$ with radius $r = 2$.
  • In 3D ($\mathbb{R}^3$): The solid region on or inside the circular cylinder centered along the $z$-axis with radius $r = 2$.
Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Identify the boundary equation.

Replacing $\le$ with $=$ gives $x^2 + y^2 = 4 = 2^2$.

Because the inequality is $\le$ (contains equality), the boundary is included and drawn as a solid circle passing through $(\pm 2, 0)$ and $(0, \pm 2)$.

Step 2: Interpret in 2D ($\mathbb{R}^2$).

In the $xy$-plane, $x^2 + y^2 \le 4$ consists of all points $(x, y)$ whose distance squared from the origin is at most $4$.

This is the closed disk on or inside the circle with center $C(0, 0)$ and radius $r = 2$.

Step 3: Interpret in 3D ($\mathbb{R}^3$).

In 3D space, the variable $z$ is missing, so $z$ can be any real number ($-\infty < z < \infty$).

The 2D disk is extruded infinitely up and down along the $z$-axis, forming the solid region on or inside the circular cylinder of radius $r = 2$ centered along the $z$-axis.

13.1 Functions of Several Variables & Level Curves

Syllabus 13.1 — Domain, Range, 3D Graphing & Level Curves (Contour Maps)

Jump to idea:
Explanation

1. Domain and Range of Functions of Two Variables

A function $f$ of two variables assigns to each ordered pair $(x, y)$ in a set $D \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ a unique real number $z = f(x, y)$.

The set $D$ is the domain of $f$, and the set of all resulting values $\{f(x, y) \mid (x, y) \in D\}$ is the range.

When finding the natural domain, apply the standard algebraic restrictions:

  • Even roots: The expression under $\sqrt{g(x, y)}$ must satisfy $g(x, y) \ge 0$.
  • Fractions: The denominator $\frac{1}{g(x, y)}$ cannot be zero: $g(x, y) \neq 0$.
  • Logarithms: The argument of $\ln(g(x, y))$ must be strictly positive: $g(x, y) > 0$.
  • Inverse trig: For $\arcsin(u)$ or $\arccos(u)$, $-1 \le u \le 1$.
Example

Worked Example 1: Finding and Graphing the Domain in the xy-Plane

Problem

Find and sketch the domain of the function:

$$f(x, y) = \frac{\sqrt{9 - x^2 - y^2}}{\ln(x + y)}$$
Final Answer

Domain: $D = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid x^2 + y^2 \le 9, \; x + y > 0, \; x + y \neq 1\}$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Identify all mathematical restrictions.

1. Square root numerator: The radicand must be non-negative:

$$9 - x^2 - y^2 \ge 0 \implies x^2 + y^2 \le 9$$

This represents the closed circular disk centered at $(0, 0)$ with radius $R = 3$ (including the boundary circle $x^2 + y^2 = 9$).

2. Natural logarithm in denominator: The argument must be strictly positive:

$$x + y > 0 \implies y > -x$$

This is the strict open half-plane strictly above the dashed line $y = -x$.

3. Non-zero denominator: The denominator cannot equal zero:

$$\ln(x + y) \neq 0 \implies x + y \neq 1 \implies y \neq -x + 1$$

This removes the entire dashed line $y = -x + 1$ from the domain.

Step 2: Combine all domain conditions.

The domain consists of all points inside and on the boundary of the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 9$ that lie strictly above the line $y = -x$, excluding the line $y = 1 - x$.

Explanation

2. Level Curves and Contour Maps

The level curves of a function $f(x, y)$ are the 2D curves with equations:

$$f(x, y) = k$$

where $k$ is a constant in the range of $f$.

Geometrically, a level curve $f(x, y) = k$ is the horizontal slice of the 3D surface $z = f(x, y)$ cut by the horizontal plane $z = k$, projected down onto the $xy$-plane.

A collection of level curves for evenly spaced values of $k$ is called a contour map.

Where contour lines are closely spaced, the surface is steep; where they are widely spaced, the surface is relatively flat.

Example

Worked Example 2: Sketching and Identifying Level Curves

Problem

Describe the level curves of the function $f(x, y) = \sqrt{16 - x^2 - 4y^2}$ for $k = 0, 2, \sqrt{12}$.

Final Answer

For $k = 0$: The ellipse $\frac{x^2}{16} + \frac{y^2}{4} = 1$.
For $k = 2$: The smaller ellipse $\frac{x^2}{12} + \frac{y^2}{3} = 1$.
For $k = \sqrt{12}$: The ellipse $\frac{x^2}{4} + y^2 = 1$.
All level curves are concentric ellipses centered at $(0, 0)$.

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Set up the level curve equation $f(x, y) = k$.

$$\sqrt{16 - x^2 - 4y^2} = k$$

Square both sides (valid for $0 \le k \le 4$):

$$16 - x^2 - 4y^2 = k^2 \implies x^2 + 4y^2 = 16 - k^2$$

Step 2: Evaluate for each given value of $k$.

1. For $k = 0$:

$$x^2 + 4y^2 = 16 \implies \frac{x^2}{16} + \frac{y^2}{4} = 1$$

This is an ellipse centered at $(0, 0)$ with semi-major axis $a = 4$ along the $x$-axis and semi-minor axis $b = 2$ along the $y$-axis.

2. For $k = 2$:

$$x^2 + 4y^2 = 16 - 2^2 = 12 \implies \frac{x^2}{12} + \frac{y^2}{3} = 1$$

This is an ellipse with semi-axes $a = \sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt{3}$ and $b = \sqrt{3}$.

3. For $k = \sqrt{12}$:

$$x^2 + 4y^2 = 16 - 12 = 4 \implies \frac{x^2}{4} + y^2 = 1$$

This is an ellipse with semi-axes $a = 2$ and $b = 1$.

13.2 Limits & Continuity of Multivariable Functions

Syllabus 13.2 — Evaluating Limits, Two-Path Test for Non-Existence & Continuity

Jump to idea:
Explanation

1. The Two-Path Test for Non-Existence of Limits

In single-variable calculus $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$, $x$ can only approach $a$ from two directions: left or right.

In multivariable calculus $\lim_{(x, y) \to (a, b)} f(x, y)$, $(x, y)$ can approach $(a, b)$ along infinitely many curves, lines, and directions.

The Two-Path Test: If $f(x, y) \to L_1$ along one path $C_1$ and $f(x, y) \to L_2$ along another path $C_2$, with $L_1 \neq L_2$, then:

$$\lim_{(x, y) \to (a, b)} f(x, y) \quad \text{does not exist (DNE)}.$$

Common test paths to check as $(x, y) \to (0, 0)$:

  • Along the $x$-axis ($y = 0$).
  • Along the $y$-axis ($x = 0$).
  • Along lines $y = mx$.
  • Along parabolas $y = kx^2$ or $x = ky^2$ (used when degree balancing is required).
Example

Worked Example 1: Evaluating Multivariable Limit at the Origin

Problem

Determine whether the limit exists or show that it does not exist:

$$\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \frac{x y^2}{x^2 + y^4}$$
Final Answer

The limit does not exist (DNE).

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Test along linear paths $y = mx$.

Substitute $y = mx$ into the limit as $x \to 0$:

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x (mx)^2}{x^2 + (mx)^4} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{m^2 x^3}{x^2(1 + m^4 x^2)} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{m^2 x}{1 + m^4 x^2} = \frac{0}{1} = 0$$

Along every straight line through the origin, the limit is $0$. However, this does not prove the limit is $0$!

Step 2: Balance the degrees in the denominator.

Notice the denominator has $x^2$ and $y^4$. If we set $x = ky^2$, then $x^2 = k^2 y^4$, making both terms in the denominator of degree $4$!

Substitute the parabolic path $x = ky^2$ as $y \to 0$:

$$\lim_{y \to 0} \frac{(k y^2) y^2}{(k y^2)^2 + y^4} = \lim_{y \to 0} \frac{k y^4}{k^2 y^4 + y^4} = \lim_{y \to 0} \frac{k}{k^2 + 1} = \frac{k}{k^2 + 1}$$

Step 3: Conclude by the Two-Path Test.

For $k = 1$ (path $x = y^2$): limit is $\frac{1}{1^2+1} = \frac{1}{2}$.

For $k = 0$ (path $x = 0$): limit is $0$.

Since different paths yield different limits ($ rac{1}{2} \neq 0$), by the Two-Path Test, the limit does not exist (DNE).

Explanation

2. Proving Limits Exist (Polar Coordinates & Squeeze Theorem)

To prove that a limit $\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} f(x, y) = L$ actually exists, we cannot just test paths. Two standard techniques are:

1. Polar Coordinates: Substitute $x = r\cos\theta, y = r\sin\theta$.

Since $(x, y) \to (0, 0) \iff r \to 0^+$, if the expression simplifies to $g(r) h(\theta)$ where $h(\theta)$ is bounded and $\lim_{r \to 0^+} g(r) = 0$, then the limit is $0$.

2. The Squeeze (Sandwich) Theorem: If $g(x, y) \le f(x, y) \le h(x, y)$ and $\lim g = \lim h = L$, then $\lim f = L$.

Example

Worked Example 2: Evaluating a Rational Multivariable Limit

Problem

Find the limit if it exists, or show that it does not exist:

$$\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \frac{3x^3 - y^3}{x^2 + y^2}$$
Final Answer

The limit exists and equals $0$.

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Convert to polar coordinates.

Set $x = r\cos\theta$ and $y = r\sin\theta$, so that $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$. As $(x, y) \to (0, 0)$, $r \to 0^+$:

$$\lim_{r \to 0^+} \frac{3(r\cos\theta)^3 - (r\sin\theta)^3}{r^2} = \lim_{r \to 0^+} \frac{r^3 (3\cos^3\theta - \sin^3\theta)}{r^2} = \lim_{r \to 0^+} r (3\cos^3\theta - \sin^3\theta)$$

Step 2: Apply the Squeeze Theorem.

Notice that $|3\cos^3\theta - \sin^3\theta| \le 3|\cos\theta|^3 + |\sin\theta|^3 \le 3(1) + 1 = 4$.

Therefore:

$$0 \le |r (3\cos^3\theta - \sin^3\theta)| \le 4r$$

As $r \to 0^+$, $4r \to 0$. By the Squeeze Theorem:

$$\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \frac{3x^3 - y^3}{x^2 + y^2} = 0$$

13.3 Partial Derivatives & Clairaut's Theorem

Syllabus 13.3 — First-Order Partials, Geometric Slopes & Mixed Second-Order Derivatives

Jump to idea:
Explanation

1. First-Order Partial Derivatives

For a function $z = f(x, y)$, the partial derivative with respect to $x$, denoted $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ or $f_x$, is found by treating $y$ as a constant and differentiating with respect to $x$:

$$f_x(x, y) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h, y) - f(x, y)}{h}$$

Similarly, the partial derivative with respect to $y$, denoted $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ or $f_y$, is found by treating $x$ as a constant and differentiating with respect to $y$:

$$f_y(x, y) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x, y + h) - f(x, y)}{h}$$

Geometric Meaning: $f_x(x_0, y_0)$ is the slope of the tangent line to the surface $z = f(x, y)$ in the plane $y = y_0$ (parallel to the $xz$-plane).

Example

Worked Example 1: Differentiating with Product & Chain Rules

Problem

Find $f_x$ and $f_y$ for the function:

$$f(x, y) = x^3 y^2 + e^{x^2 y} + \sin(2x - 3y)$$
Final Answer

$f_x = 3x^2 y^2 + 2xy e^{x^2 y} + 2\cos(2x - 3y)$
$f_y = 2x^3 y + x^2 e^{x^2 y} - 3\cos(2x - 3y)$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Compute $f_x$ (treat $y$ as a constant).

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^3 y^2) = 3x^2 y^2$$$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(e^{x^2 y}) = e^{x^2 y} \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2 y) = 2xy e^{x^2 y}$$$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\sin(2x - 3y)) = \cos(2x - 3y) \cdot 2 = 2\cos(2x - 3y)$$$$f_x = 3x^2 y^2 + 2xy e^{x^2 y} + 2\cos(2x - 3y)$$

Step 2: Compute $f_y$ (treat $x$ as a constant).

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^3 y^2) = 2x^3 y$$$$\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(e^{x^2 y}) = e^{x^2 y} \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^2 y) = x^2 e^{x^2 y}$$$$\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(\sin(2x - 3y)) = \cos(2x - 3y) \cdot (-3) = -3\cos(2x - 3y)$$$$f_y = 2x^3 y + x^2 e^{x^2 y} - 3\cos(2x - 3y)$$

Explanation

2. Second-Order Partial Derivatives & Clairaut's Theorem

A function $f(x, y)$ has four second-order partial derivatives:

$$f_{xx} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}, \quad f_{yy} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}, \quad f_{xy} = (f_x)_y = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y \partial x}, \quad f_{yx} = (f_y)_x = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \partial y}$$

Clairaut's Theorem (Equality of Mixed Partials): If $f(x, y)$ and its partial derivatives $f_x, f_y, f_{xy}, f_{yx}$ are continuous on an open disk containing $(a, b)$, then:

$$f_{xy}(a, b) = f_{yx}(a, b)$$
Example

Worked Example 2: Verifying Mixed Partials Equality

Problem

Verify that $f_{xy} = f_{yx}$ for $f(x, y) = x \ln(x + 2y)$.

Final Answer

$f_{xy} = f_{yx} = \frac{2}{(x + 2y)^2}$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Compute first partials $f_x$ and $f_y$.

$$f_x = 1 \cdot \ln(x + 2y) + x \cdot \frac{1}{x + 2y} = \ln(x + 2y) + \frac{x}{x + 2y}$$$$f_y = x \cdot \frac{2}{x + 2y} = \frac{2x}{x + 2y}$$

Step 2: Differentiate $f_x$ with respect to $y$ to get $f_{xy}$.

$$f_{xy} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\ln(x + 2y) + x(x + 2y)^{-1}\right) = \frac{2}{x + 2y} - x(x + 2y)^{-2}(2) = \frac{2(x + 2y) - 2x}{(x + 2y)^2} = \frac{4y}{(x + 2y)^2}$$

Step 3: Differentiate $f_y$ with respect to $x$ to get $f_{yx}$.

$$f_{yx} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{2x}{x + 2y}\right) = \frac{2(x + 2y) - 2x(1)}{(x + 2y)^2} = \frac{2x + 4y - 2x}{(x + 2y)^2} = \frac{4y}{(x + 2y)^2}$$

Since both equal $\frac{4y}{(x + 2y)^2}$, Clairaut's Theorem is verified!

13.4 Differentials & Linear Approximations

Syllabus 13.4 — Total Differential, Tangent Plane Approximations & Error Estimation

Jump to idea:
Explanation

1. Linear Approximation & The Total Differential

The linear approximation (or tangent plane approximation) of a differentiable function $f(x, y)$ near $(x_0, y_0)$ is:

$$L(x, y) = f(x_0, y_0) + f_x(x_0, y_0)(x - x_0) + f_y(x_0, y_0)(y - y_0)$$

The total differential $dz$ is defined in terms of the independent differentials $dx = \Delta x$ and $dy = \Delta y$ by:

$$dz = f_x(x, y) dx + f_y(x, y) dy = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} dx + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} dy$$

For small changes $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$, the true increment $\Delta z = f(x_0 + \Delta x, y_0 + \Delta y) - f(x_0, y_0)$ is closely approximated by the differential:

$$\Delta z \approx dz$$
Example

Worked Example 1: Estimating Values via Tangent Plane Approximation

Problem

Use linear approximation to estimate the value of:

$$f(x, y) = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \quad \text{at } (3.04, \; 3.98)$$
Final Answer

$L(3.04, 3.98) = 5.008$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Choose the convenient base point $(x_0, y_0)$.

Select $(x_0, y_0) = (3, 4)$, where $f(3, 4) = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.

Step 2: Determine $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$.

$$dx = \Delta x = 3.04 - 3 = 0.04$$$$dy = \Delta y = 3.98 - 4 = -0.02$$

Step 3: Compute first partial derivatives at $(3, 4)$.

$$f_x(x, y) = \frac{2x}{2\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}} = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}} \implies f_x(3, 4) = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6$$$$f_y(x, y) = \frac{2y}{2\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}} = \frac{y}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}} \implies f_y(3, 4) = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8$$

Step 4: Calculate $dz$ and the approximation.

$$dz = f_x \Delta x + f_y \Delta y = (0.6)(0.04) + (0.8)(-0.02) = 0.024 - 0.016 = 0.008$$$$f(3.04, 3.98) \approx f(3, 4) + dz = 5 + 0.008 = 5.008$$

13.5 The Multivariable Chain Rule

Syllabus 13.5 — Tree Diagrams, Intermediate Variables & Implicit Differentiation Formula

Jump to idea:
Explanation

1. The Multivariable Chain Rule & Tree Diagrams

Case 1 (One Independent Variable): If $z = f(x, y)$ is differentiable and $x = g(t), y = h(t)$ are differentiable functions of $t$, then:

$$\frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} \frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} \frac{dy}{dt}$$

Case 2 (Two Independent Variables): If $z = f(x, y)$ where $x = g(s, t)$ and $y = h(s, t)$, then:

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial s} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} \frac{\partial x}{\partial s} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} \frac{\partial y}{\partial s}, \quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} \frac{\partial x}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} \frac{\partial y}{\partial t}$$

Rule of Thumb (Tree Diagram): Sum over all paths from the top dependent variable to the target independent variable, multiplying along each branch.

Example

Worked Example 1: Multivariable Chain Rule with Two Parameters

Problem

Given $z = x^2 y - y^3$, where $x = s \cos t$ and $y = s \sin t$, find $\frac{\partial z}{\partial s}$ and $\frac{\partial z}{\partial t}$ at $(s, t) = (2, 0)$.

Final Answer

At $(s, t) = (2, 0)$: $\frac{\partial z}{\partial s} = 0$, and $\frac{\partial z}{\partial t} = 8$.

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Evaluate $x$ and $y$ at $(s, t) = (2, 0)$.

$$x = 2\cos(0) = 2, \quad y = 2\sin(0) = 0$$

Step 2: Compute partials of $z$ at $(x, y) = (2, 0)$.

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = 2xy \implies \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}(2, 0) = 2(2)(0) = 0$$$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = x^2 - 3y^2 \implies \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}(2, 0) = 2^2 - 3(0)^2 = 4$$

Step 3: Compute partials of $x$ and $y$ with respect to $s$ and $t$.

$$\frac{\partial x}{\partial s} = \cos t = \cos(0) = 1, \quad \frac{\partial y}{\partial s} = \sin t = \sin(0) = 0$$$$\frac{\partial x}{\partial t} = -s\sin t = -2\sin(0) = 0, \quad \frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = s\cos t = 2\cos(0) = 2$$

Step 4: Apply Chain Rule formulas.

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial s} = (0)(1) + (4)(0) = 0$$$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial t} = (0)(0) + (4)(2) = 8$$
Explanation

2. Implicit Differentiation Shortcut Formula

If an equation $F(x, y) = 0$ implicitly defines $y$ as a differentiable function of $x$, then differentiating implicitly yields:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{F_x}{F_y} \quad (F_y \neq 0)$$

Similarly, if $F(x, y, z) = 0$ defines $z$ as a function of $x$ and $y$ ($z = f(x, y)$), then:

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -\frac{F_x}{F_z}, \quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = -\frac{F_y}{F_z} \quad (F_z \neq 0)$$
Example

Worked Example 2: Finding Partial Derivatives of an Implicit Surface

Problem

Find $\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}$ and $\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}$ for the implicit surface equation:

$$x^3 + y^3 + z^3 + 6xyz = 1$$
Final Answer

$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -\frac{x^2 + 2yz}{z^2 + 2xy}$, $\quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = -\frac{y^2 + 2xz}{z^2 + 2xy}$

Step-by-Step Solution

$$F(x, y, z) = x^3 + y^3 + z^3 + 6xyz - 1 = 0$$

Step 2: Compute first partials of $F$.

$$F_x = 3x^2 + 6yz = 3(x^2 + 2yz)$$$$F_y = 3y^2 + 6xz = 3(y^2 + 2xz)$$$$F_z = 3z^2 + 6xy = 3(z^2 + 2xy)$$

Step 3: Apply the implicit differentiation formulas.

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -\frac{F_x}{F_z} = -\frac{3(x^2 + 2yz)}{3(z^2 + 2xy)} = -\frac{x^2 + 2yz}{z^2 + 2xy}$$$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = -\frac{F_y}{F_z} = -\frac{3(y^2 + 2xz)}{3(z^2 + 2xy)} = -\frac{y^2 + 2xz}{z^2 + 2xy}$$

13.6 Directional Derivatives & Gradients

Syllabus 13.6 — The Gradient Vector, Directional Derivative in Any Direction & Maximum Rate of Change

Jump to idea:
Explanation

1. The Gradient Vector & Directional Derivative Formula

The gradient of a function $f(x, y)$ is the vector function:

$$\nabla f(x, y) = \langle f_x(x, y), \; f_y(x, y) \rangle = f_x \mathbf{i} + f_y \mathbf{j}$$

The directional derivative of $f$ at $(x_0, y_0)$ in the direction of a unit vector $\mathbf{u} = \langle u_1, u_2 \rangle$ is the rate of change of $f$ in that direction:

$$D_{\mathbf{u}} f(x_0, y_0) = \nabla f(x_0, y_0) \cdot \mathbf{u} = f_x(x_0, y_0) u_1 + f_y(x_0, y_0) u_2$$

Critical Rule: The direction vector MUST be normalized to a unit vector $\mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}$ before taking the dot product!

Example

Worked Example 1: Directional Derivative in the Direction of a Vector

Problem

Find the directional derivative of $f(x, y) = x^2 y^3 - 4y$ at $P(2, -1)$ in the direction of $\mathbf{v} = 2\mathbf{i} + 5\mathbf{j}$.

Final Answer

$D_{\mathbf{u}} f(2, -1) = \frac{28}{\sqrt{29}}$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Compute the gradient vector $\nabla f(x, y)$.

$$f_x(x, y) = 2x y^3 \implies f_x(2, -1) = 2(2)(-1)^3 = -4$$$$f_y(x, y) = 3x^2 y^2 - 4 \implies f_y(2, -1) = 3(2)^2(-1)^2 - 4 = 12 - 4 = 8$$$$\nabla f(2, -1) = \langle -4, 8 \rangle$$

Step 2: Find the unit vector $\mathbf{u}$ in the direction of $\mathbf{v}$.

$$\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{2^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{4 + 25} = \sqrt{29}$$$$\mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|} = \left\langle \frac{2}{\sqrt{29}}, \; \frac{5}{\sqrt{29}} \right\rangle$$

Step 3: Calculate the dot product $D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$.

$$D_{\mathbf{u}} f(2, -1) = (-4)\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{29}}\right) + (8)\left(\frac{5}{\sqrt{29}}\right) = \frac{-8 + 40}{\sqrt{29}} = \frac{32}{\sqrt{29}}$$

Explanation

2. Maximizing the Directional Derivative

By the geometric dot product: $D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \|\nabla f\| \|\mathbf{u}\| \cos\theta = \|\nabla f\| \cos\theta$.

Since $-1 \le \cos\theta \le 1$:

  • Maximum Rate of Change: Occurs when $\cos\theta = 1$ (same direction as $\nabla f$). Its value is $\|\nabla f(x_0, y_0)\|$.
  • Minimum Rate of Change (Steepest Descent): Occurs when $\cos\theta = -1$ (direction of $-\nabla f$). Its value is $-\|\nabla f(x_0, y_0)\|$.
  • Zero Rate of Change: Occurs in directions orthogonal to $\nabla f$ (tangent to the level curve).
Example

Worked Example 2: Finding Maximum Rate of Change and its Direction

Problem

Given $f(x, y, z) = \frac{x}{y} + \frac{y}{z}$ at the point $P(4, 2, 1)$:

  1. Find the maximum rate of change of $f$ at $P$.
  2. Find the direction in which this maximum rate of change occurs.
Final Answer

Direction: $\mathbf{u} = \left\langle \frac{1}{5}, -\frac{3}{10}, -\frac{4}{5} \right\rangle$
Maximum Rate of Change: $\|\nabla f\| = \frac{\sqrt{101}}{4} \approx 2.51$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Compute the gradient $\nabla f(x, y, z)$.

$$f_x = \frac{1}{y} \implies f_x(4, 2, 1) = \frac{1}{2}$$

$$f_y = -\frac{x}{y^2} + \frac{1}{z} \implies f_y(4, 2, 1) = -\frac{4}{4} + \frac{1}{1} = -1 + 1 = 0$$

$$f_z = -\frac{y}{z^2} \implies f_z(4, 2, 1) = -\frac{2}{1} = -2$$

$$\nabla f(4, 2, 1) = \langle 0.5, \; 0, \; -2 \rangle = \left\langle \frac{1}{2}, 0, -2 \right\rangle$$

Step 2: Maximum rate of change.

$$\|\nabla f\| = \sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 + 0^2 + (-2)^2} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{4} + 4} = \sqrt{\frac{17}{4}} = \frac{\sqrt{17}}{2}$$

Step 3: Direction of maximum change (unit vector in gradient direction).

$$\mathbf{u} = \frac{\nabla f}{\|\nabla f\|} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{17}} \left\langle \frac{1}{2}, 0, -2 \right\rangle = \left\langle \frac{1}{\sqrt{17}}, 0, -\frac{4}{\sqrt{17}} \right\rangle$$

13.7 Tangent Planes & Normal Lines to Surfaces

Syllabus 13.7 — Gradient as Normal Vector, Tangent Plane Equations & Normal Lines to Level Surfaces

Jump to idea:
Explanation

1. Tangent Planes and Normal Lines to Level Surfaces F(x, y, z) = k

For any smooth surface represented as a level surface $F(x, y, z) = k$, the gradient vector $\nabla F(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ is always perpendicular (normal) to the tangent plane of the surface at $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$.

Therefore, the normal vector is:

$$\mathbf{n} = \nabla F(x_0, y_0, z_0) = \langle F_x(x_0, y_0, z_0), \; F_y(x_0, y_0, z_0), \; F_z(x_0, y_0, z_0) \rangle$$

Equation of the Tangent Plane:

$$F_x(x_0, y_0, z_0)(x - x_0) + F_y(x_0, y_0, z_0)(y - y_0) + F_z(x_0, y_0, z_0)(z - z_0) = 0$$

Symmetric Equations of the Normal Line:

$$\frac{x - x_0}{F_x(x_0, y_0, z_0)} = \frac{y - y_0}{F_y(x_0, y_0, z_0)} = \frac{z - z_0}{F_z(x_0, y_0, z_0)}$$

Example

Worked Example 1: Tangent Plane and Normal Line to a Surface

Problem

Find the equations of the tangent plane and the normal line to the ellipsoid $\frac{x^2}{4} + y^2 + \frac{z^2}{9} = 3$ at the point $P(-2, 1, -3)$.

Final Answer

Tangent plane: $-x + 2y - \frac{2}{3}z - 6 = 0 \iff 3x - 6y + 2z + 18 = 0$
Normal line: $\frac{x + 2}{-1} = \frac{y - 1}{2} = \frac{z + 3}{-2/3}$

Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Define the level surface function $F(x, y, z)$.

$$F(x, y, z) = \frac{x^2}{4} + y^2 + \frac{z^2}{9} - 3 = 0$$

Step 2: Compute the gradient vector $\nabla F$ at $P(-2, 1, -3)$.

$$F_x = \frac{2x}{4} = \frac{x}{2} \implies F_x(-2, 1, -3) = -1$$$$F_y = 2y \implies F_y(-2, 1, -3) = 2(1) = 2$$$$F_z = \frac{2z}{9} \implies F_z(-2, 1, -3) = -\frac{6}{9} = -\frac{2}{3}$$

The normal vector is $\mathbf{n} = \left\langle -1, 2, -\frac{2}{3} \right\rangle$, or multiplying by $-3$: $\mathbf{n} = \langle 3, -6, 2 \rangle$.

Step 3: Write the equation of the tangent plane.

$$3(x - (-2)) - 6(y - 1) + 2(z - (-3)) = 0$$$$3(x + 2) - 6(y - 1) + 2(z + 3) = 0$$$$3x + 6 - 6y + 6 + 2z + 6 = 0 \implies 3x - 6y + 2z + 18 = 0$$

Step 4: Write the symmetric equations of the normal line.

$$\frac{x + 2}{3} = \frac{y - 1}{-6} = \frac{z + 3}{2}$$

Crucial limit evaluation skill — applying polar substitution and Squeeze Theorem to prove a limit equals 0.
Problem Statement

Select Option:
Boxed Final Answer
Correct Option: (D)

$\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \dfrac{x^2 y}{x^2 + y^2} = 0$.

**Method 1: Polar Coordinates**
Let $x = r \cos\theta$ and $y = r \sin\theta$. As $(x, y) \to (0, 0)$, $r \to 0^+$:$$\frac{x^2 y}{x^2 + y^2} = \frac{(r^2 \cos^2\theta)(r \sin\theta)}{r^2} = r \cos^2\theta \sin\theta$$Since $|\cos^2\theta \sin\theta| \le 1$, we have:$$|r \cos^2\theta \sin\theta| \le r \to 0 \quad \text{as } r \to 0^+$$By the Squeeze Theorem, the limit is $0$, regardless of direction $\theta$.
**Method 2: Squeeze Inequality**
Notice that $\dfrac{x^2}{x^2 + y^2} \le 1$ for all $(x, y) \ne (0, 0)$. Therefore:$$0 \le \left| \frac{x^2 y}{x^2 + y^2} \right| = \frac{x^2}{x^2 + y^2} |y| \le 1 \cdot |y| = |y|$$Since $\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} |y| = 0$, by the Squeeze Theorem:$$\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \frac{x^2 y}{x^2 + y^2} = 0$$

Correct Option: (A)

$$z = x^2 - (x + 1)^2$$
Step 3: Expand the Binomial and Simplify
$$z = x^2 - (x^2 + 2x + 1) = x^2 - x^2 - 2x - 1 = -2x - 1$$

Practice Test 1 • Q7 Medium 13.2 Limits & Continuity

Evaluating Limits via Polar Coordinates & Bounding

Crucial limit evaluation skill — applying polar substitution and Squeeze Theorem to prove a limit equals 0.
Problem Statement

Evaluate the limit:$$\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \frac{x^2 y}{x^2 + y^2}$$

Select Option:
Boxed Final Answer
Correct Option: (D)

$\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \dfrac{x^2 y}{x^2 + y^2} = 0$.

**Method 1: Polar Coordinates**
Let $x = r \cos\theta$ and $y = r \sin\theta$. As $(x, y) \to (0, 0)$, $r \to 0^+$:$$\frac{x^2 y}{x^2 + y^2} = \frac{(r^2 \cos^2\theta)(r \sin\theta)}{r^2} = r \cos^2\theta \sin\theta$$Since $|\cos^2\theta \sin\theta| \le 1$, we have:$$|r \cos^2\theta \sin\theta| \le r \to 0 \quad \text{as } r \to 0^+$$By the Squeeze Theorem, the limit is $0$, regardless of direction $\theta$.
**Method 2: Squeeze Inequality**
Notice that $\dfrac{x^2}{x^2 + y^2} \le 1$ for all $(x, y) \ne (0, 0)$. Therefore:$$0 \le \left| \frac{x^2 y}{x^2 + y^2} \right| = \frac{x^2}{x^2 + y^2} |y| \le 1 \cdot |y| = |y|$$Since $\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} |y| = 0$, by the Squeeze Theorem:$$\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \frac{x^2 y}{x^2 + y^2} = 0$$
Practice Test 1 • Q8 Medium-Hard 13.2 Two-Path Test

Proving Non-Existence of a Limit Along Degree-Balanced Paths

Classic KFUPM trap — straight lines yield 0, but parabolic paths $y = kx^2$ expose non-existence.
Problem Statement

Evaluate the limit:$$\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \frac{x^2 y}{x^4 + y^2}$$

Select Option:
Boxed Final Answer
Correct Option: (A)

The limit Does Not Exist (DNE) by the Two-Path Test.

Step 1: Test Along Straight Lines $y = mx$
$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x^2(mx)}{x^4 + (mx)^2} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{m x^3}{x^2(x^2 + m^2)} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{m x}{x^2 + m^2} = 0 \quad (\text{for } m \ne 0)$$Along the coordinate axes ($x = 0$ or $y = 0$), the expression is also identically $0$.
Step 2: Examine Degree Balance in the Denominator
In $x^4 + y^2$, the terms balance when $y \sim x^2$. Let us test parabolic paths $y = k x^2$:$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x^2(k x^2)}{x^4 + (k x^2)^2} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{k x^4}{x^4 + k^2 x^4} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{k x^4}{x^4(1 + k^2)} = \frac{k}{1 + k^2}$$
Step 3: Evaluate Different Paths
  • Along $y = x^2$ ($k = 1$): $\dfrac{1}{1 + 1^2} = \dfrac{1}{2}$
  • Along $y = -x^2$ ($k = -1$): $\dfrac{-1}{1 + (-1)^2} = -\dfrac{1}{2}$
  • Along $y = 0$ ($k = 0$): $0$
Since different paths produce different limiting values, the limit **Does Not Exist (DNE)**.

Select Option:
Boxed Final Answer
Correct Option: (D)

The volume increases by approximately $3\%$.

Step 1: Formula for Volume and Total Differential
Volume of the box is $V = xyz$. The total differential is:$$dV = \frac{\partial V}{\partial x} dx + \frac{\partial V}{\partial y} dy + \frac{\partial V}{\partial z} dz = (yz) dx + (xz) dy + (xy) dz$$
Step 2: Express Relative Percentage Change

Problem Statement

Let $z = x^2 y + e^{xy}$, with $x = s + t$ and $y = s - t$. Find $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial t}$ at the parameter point $(s, t) = (1, 0)$:

Select Option:
Boxed Final Answer
Correct Option: (A)

$\left. \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial t} \right|_{(1, 0)} = 1$.

Finding the Maximum Directional Derivative via Gradient Magnitude

High frequency concept — the maximum rate of change at point $P$ is equal to $\| abla f(P)\|$.
Problem Statement

For the surface $f(x, y) = x^2 + 2xy + 3y^2$, find the maximum directional derivative at the point $(1, -1)$:

Select Option:
Boxed Final Answer
Correct Option: (B)

The maximum directional derivative at $(1, -1)$ is $4$.

For any differentiable function $f$, the maximum value of the directional derivative $D_{\mathbf{u}} f$ at a point $P$ occurs when $\mathbf{u}$ has the direction of $\nabla f(P)$, and its maximum value is:$$D_{\mathbf{u}, \max} f(P) = \|\nabla f(P)\|$$
Step 2: Calculate the Gradient Vector $\nabla f(x, y)$

Step 4: Compute the Length of the Gradient Vector
$$\|\nabla f(1, -1)\| = \sqrt{0^2 + (-4)^2} = \sqrt{16} = 4$$
